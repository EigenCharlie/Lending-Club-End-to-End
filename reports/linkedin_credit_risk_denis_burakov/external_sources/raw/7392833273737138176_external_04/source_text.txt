Data science is not only about predictions and classification. It has "data" and "science" in name, so other elements of analysis such as inference, estimation, causal analysis, etc are part of it as well.
One of the regression methods often mentioned by data scientists, logistic regression is often referred to as "classification only", described typically like "despite its confusing name...".
In statistics, on which data science is based, there's nothing confusing about this name, it's a core regression algorithm 🤷‍♂️
➡️ by its history: invented for (surprise) regression purposes years before used in ML,
➡️ by statistical considerations about conditional expectation - the core of Cox, McFadden, Berkson, Nelder and Wedderburn's work on the Generalized Linear Model, where the linear, Poisson, logistic, gamma and several other special cases follow same logic, differing just by the distribution of conditional response and link
➡️ by numerous applications in research, including advanced hypothesis testing, analysis of model effects, implementing matching and weighting algorithms, detecting missingness patterns, etc.
It's worth knowing to not limit ourselves when switching domain, for example in experimental research, like clinical trials.
Let me say, I've never used it for classification while using it at work on daily basis for testing hypotheses about treatment efficacy and safety.
💡Remember and share with others: Classification may be the only application in ML but only there.

The biggest challenge with rank test (Mann-Whitney/Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Brunner-Munzel, Fligner-Pocello, ATS, WTS, etc) is its null hypothesis: stochastic superiority. It's neither about means nor medians, nor eCDFs in general (like Anderson-Darling would do).
It means that unless strong distributional assumptions hold, the IID + symmetry around medians we are unable to say how two samples differ: by means? Medians? Variances? Skewness? Modes?
It's very easy to obtain p>0.999 for very different means or medians and p<0.00..01 for equal means or medians even for small samples.
Stochastic superiority may be able to detect most kind of changes (in shape, dispersion, central tendency) but won't tell you what precisely. If you need to look at the details, consider quantile regression at different quantiles. This will tell how the data "behave" at different place of their distribution and will miss fewer details and keep the interpretation.
Rank-based methods are OK for ordinal data or if the nature of difference and the magnitudes can be ignored entirely.
If, despite non-normally looking samples, you need to compare means (if it's meaningful) - the permutation Welch t, Freedman-Lane ANOVA, or wild bootstrap with Rademacher weights ANOVA will compares means without normality: https://lnkd.in/dA5ZGdHW

I read yeaterday: "That’s why you should embrace non-parametric statistics and start loving ranks 🙂". This is in spirit "Non-parametric = no problems" claim, which I hardly disagree with if made generally.
Think about it.
What hypothesis will we assess? You lose simple interpretation in most cases.
Let's take the Mann-Whitney(-Wilcoxon): it's not about means, not about medians, not even entire distributions. It's about stochastic superiority and this is not something we easily use, say, in clinical non-inferiority or bioequivalence studies.
Ranks have no connection to any common measure unless a strong (and often unrealistic assumption) holds: identically distributed, leading to shift-location model.
And even this isn't fully true for Mann-Whitney(-Wilcoxon), because if variances are different (which happens almost always), the type-1 error is compromised.
So yes, IT DOES make some assumption 😉
And we need then the Brunner-Munzel just like Welch-Satterthwaite for the t-test case.
But why not employing a distribution-free test that preserve a meaningful outcome, e.g. expressed in means (assuming they DO make sense for such data) like permutation Welch t test, Freedman-Lane AN(C)OVA, wild bootstrap AN(C)OVA? Or quantile regression maybe, giving you well-interpretable outcomes?
This is important for example in my field - clinical trials, where the key analyses must be planned in advance and use concrete estimators aligned with the clinical question. If comparison of means for clinical superiority testing was planned, switching to a rank-based test changes that. In other words, you obtain a technically valid answer to a never asked question.
/ *it' s easy to find cases where p < 0.0..01 for equal means or medians at low N, and p>0.999 for different means or medians - even at large N. /
Maybe you'll be very lucky and the IID holds + also the distributions are close to Gaussian - but then hey! Probably either version of the CLT would do the job fine, without even reaching for ranks and lowering power or compromising Type-1 error (so important in clinical trials).
Rank-based methods like DO have their deserved place in case of difficult distributions (where a single-number summary doesn't suffice) or ordinal data, but at the cost of harder interpretation.
For ordinal data stochastic superiority (probabilist index) is the most useful information we can obtain, but for numerical data? We can do much better!

This post is about the different uses of the word "marginal" in economics versus in statistics.
In economics, we have expression like "marginal cost" and "marginal utility" and it refers to "marginal" in the sense of "incremental". It is a margin in the sense that an onion is composed of many layers and you can peel off the outermost layer, lying at the outmost edge or periphery.
Mathematically the onion picture corresponds to differentiation (ie differencing). Marginal cost and marginal utility are essentially rates of change at a given level.
Statisticians use the term "marginal distribution" or "marginal probabilities". It comes from the idea that if you have a two dimensional random variable that takes discrete values, then you have a table (array) of numbers (probabilities) that sum up to one. If you sum across each rows (resp. column) you can write the aggregated probabilities along the “margin” of the table.
Mathematically, this sense of margin corresponds to integration (i.e. summation). "Marginalizing" a random variable is to integrate it out of the joint distribution.
Therefore, depending on who you are talking among, "margin" is its own mathematical antonym!

The Bucharest International Clinical Trials Symposium
672 followers
Global Experts Convene in Bucharest to Advance Patient‑Centric Clinical Trials and EU Harmonisation
Bucharest, Romania – 14 November 2025 – The Bucharest International Clinical Trials Symposium (BICS) 2025 brought together regulators, researchers, industry executives and patient advocates to shape the future of clinical research. Now in its 17th year, the Clinical Trials Symposium is recognised as one of Europe’s most important forums and Romania’s leading platform for advancing harmonisation, innovation and patient‑centricity.
Hosted by the Avantyo Institute of Clinical Research, the symposium highlighted the urgent need to modernise trial design, reduce regulatory complexity and strengthen collaboration across borders. “Clinical research must evolve to be more transparent, collaborative and patient‑focused. BICS continues to serve as a vital forum for shaping this transformation,” said Dr Cristina Florescu Moraid, President of BICS and Director of Avantyo Institute of Clinical Research.
Key themes included the unveiling of the final ICH GCP E6 (R3) Guideline, new models for patient enrolment and decentralised trials, and stronger sponsor–site–patient partnerships. “Patients must be active partners, not passive participants,” said Rozalina Lăpădatu, President of the Autoimmune Patients Association (APAA). “Rebuilding trust in clinical research depends on transparent sponsor–site–patient relationships, with clear communication, predictable processes and co‑designed studies that place patients at the centre,” said Dr Alina Codrescu, Director of Clinical Operations, Balkan Cluster, Bristol Myers Squibb.
Organisers also announced the upcoming IMPACT Conference 2026, dedicated to patients as active partners in clinical research, scheduled for April 2026 in Romania.
About BICS The Bucharest International Clinical Trials Symposium (BICS) is one of Europe’s most important forums for clinical research and Romania’s leading symposium in the field. Founded in 2008 as the Clinical Trials Symposium, it has been guided from the very beginning by Dr Cristina Florescu Moraid and proudly hosted under Avantyo’s leadership since 2019. Over its 17‑year history, BICS has championed modernisation and internationalisation of clinical trials, with a strong focus on patient‑centricity and EU harmonisation.

"What (NOT) to do with (NON-) “null” results" is an excellent series in statistics written by Dr Mircea Zloteanu. I recommend it to everyone who's dealing with (frequentist) testing hypotheses in "difficult scenarios".
Why do I recommend it?
Well, because:
a) it's truly an eye-opener,
b) such content is relatively rare and poorly advertised in social media,
c) it covers important cases,
d) most -if not all- of us faced either of these problems at least once in our work. We searched for advices but either we hesitated to ask or there was nobody to ask...
It consists of 3 parts:
1️⃣ What to do with "null" results - Part I: Nonsignificant and underpowered: https://lnkd.in/d5xRmc4c
2️⃣ What to do with "null" results - Part II: Nonsignificant but with sufficient data: https://lnkd.in/dYStx9Kp
3️⃣ What NOT to do with NON-“null” results Part III: Underpowered study, but significant results: https://lnkd.in/dXJYZJfb
Of course there's more to read on prof. Zloteanu's blog, so check it out too.
