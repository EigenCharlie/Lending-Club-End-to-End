🔹 Revolutionising Fraud Detection in Digital Payments – NPCI’s G-TED SAM 🔹
India’s UPI has become a lifeline for everyday payments — billions of transactions every month, all processed in a fraction of a second. But with this speed and scale comes a huge challenge: how do you keep fraudsters out without slowing the system down?
That’s where NPCI’s latest innovation, G-TED SAM (Graph Transformer Distillation to Simple Attention Model), comes in.
🧑‍🏫 Think of it like a teacher and student:
• The Teacher model is a brilliant detective — it studies the full web of accounts and transactions to spot hidden fraud rings.
• The Student model learns from this teacher and applies those instincts instantly, checking transactions at lightning speed without needing the entire web.
💡 The impact?
• Fraud caught more accurately, without delays.
• Scalable to billions of payments.
• Affordable and deployable even for smaller banks and fintechs.
• Trust in UPI stays intact — secure and seamless.
👉 Great example of India building world-class AI for real-world financial challenges. Extending the above when Telecom Meets Payments: A New Frontline Against Fraud
Digital India’s backbone runs on two rails:
📱 Telecom — every SIM, every OTP, every device.
💳 Payments — UPI, cards, wallets, and billions of transactions a month.
Fraudsters know this. They don’t attack one system in isolation — they exploit the weak links across both. A fake SIM, a compromised KYC, a mule account… and suddenly the fraud shows up in UPI or card payments.
This is why two innovations together could be game-changing:
• DoT’s Financial Resource Intelligence (FRI): spotting fraudulent telecom identities and abnormal SIM behaviour.
• NPCI’s G-TED SAM: an AI-powered fraud detection system that learns patterns of suspicious transactions at scale, without slowing down UPI’s lightning speed.
💡 Imagine combining the two:
• FRI flags a SIM involved in suspicious activity.
• That signal feeds into G-TED SAM’s fraud graph, enriching it with telecom intelligence.
• Now NPCI can catch fraud before the money even moves — spotting mule networks and scam rings at the root.
👉 In simple terms: FRI is the neighbourhood watch outside the bank; G-TED SAM is the CCTV inside the bank. Together, they make fraudsters visible from the moment they step into the lane.

If you’re in credit risk or data analytics, you’ve likely heard of PD, LGD, and EAD — key pillars of credit risk modeling under Basel and RBI guidelines.
1. PD – Probability of Default
Definition:
PD measures how likely a customer is to default on their credit obligation (i.e., fail to make payments for a specified period, usually 90 days past due).
In credit card terms:
It’s the probability that a customer will not pay their minimum due or total due for a prolonged period and becomes a non-performing asset (NPA).
Example:
Let’s say Ravi, an Indian salaried person working in Bengaluru, has an HBCD Credit Card with a credit limit of ₹1,00,000.
He has been paying on time, but recently his company downsized, and he lost his job.
Based on the bank’s risk model and Ravi’s profile (e.g., income, utilization rate, delinquency history), the model assigns a PD = 5% (0.05).
➤ Meaning there’s a 5% chance Ravi will default in the next 12 months.
💸 2. LGD – Loss Given Default
Definition:
LGD represents the portion of exposure that the bank will lose if the customer defaults, after considering recoveries (through collections, settlements, or sale to recovery agencies).
In credit card terms:
It’s the percentage of outstanding balance the bank cannot recover after default.
Example:
Ravi’s outstanding balance at default = ₹80,000
The bank manages to recover ₹20,000 through collections over time.
So, Loss = ₹80,000 - ₹20,000 = ₹60,000
Therefore,
👉 LGD = 60,000 / 80,000 = 75%
That means the bank loses 75% of the exposure after all recoveries.
💳 3. EAD – Exposure at Default
Definition:
EAD is the total amount exposed to risk when the customer defaults — i.e., the outstanding principal + interest + accrued charges on the card at the time of default.
In credit card terms:
It’s the balance on the card (plus any pending fees/interest) when the customer stops paying.
Example:
Credit limit = ₹1,00,000
Utilized amount at time of default = ₹80,000
No further usage after delinquency
So, EAD = ₹80,000
🧮 Putting it Together – Expected Loss (EL)
Banks combine these three parameters to estimate the expected credit loss (ECL):
PD = 5% (0.05)
LGD = 75% (0.75)
EAD = ₹80,000
👉 The bank expects to lose ₹3,000 on average from customers with similar profiles.
Bank calculate these metrics for regulatory and internal risk management under frameworks such as:
Basel II / III guidelines, and
RBI’s IRB (Internal Ratings-Based) approach for credit risk.
These help:
-Set credit limits
-Decide interest rates or APR
-Estimate provisions (ECL) for IFRS 9 / Ind-AS 109 accounting
-Track portfolio-level risk trends

Verhoeff Checksum : The Math That Secures India's Identity
The 12-digit Aadhaar number, the foundation of India's digital identity system, is a masterclass in combining randomization with mathematical integrity.
The Aadhaar number is composed of two distinct parts, each serving a critical function:
The First 11 Digits (The Identifier): These digits are the actual Unique Identification Number. The Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) has confirmed that these digits are intentionally and randomly generated. They contain no embedded intelligence, no information about the holder's demographics. This randomization is the primary security feature, making the number un-guessable and ensuring it is a true, unique identifier. The number never starts with 0 or 1.
The 12th Digit (The Checksum): This is the Verhoeff Checksum, a single digit calculated from the preceding 11 digits. Its sole purpose is to act as a powerful error detection mechanism.
Why the Verhoeff Checksum?
The Verhoeff algorithm, developed by Dutch mathematician Jacobus Verhoeff in 1969, is significantly more robust than simpler checksums like the Luhn algorithm (used for credit cards). It is based on abstract algebra, specifically the Dihedral Group D5, and is designed to catch the most common human data entry errors:
Error Type - Detection Rate
Single Digit Errors (e.g., 1234 -> 1235) - 100%
Adjacent Transposition Errors (e.g., 1234 -> 1324) - 100%
Other Transposition and Twin Errors - Very High
The algorithm's power comes from its use of three pre-defined mathematical tables, which are applied sequentially to the digits of the number:
1. Dihedral Group D5 Table (Multiplication): This table defines a non-commutative "multiplication" operation on the digits 0-9. The non-commutative property is what allows the algorithm to detect transpositions
2. Permutation Table: This table dictates the order in which the digits are processed, ensuring that the position of each digit affects the final result.
3. Inverse Table: This table is used to calculate the final checksum digit.
The final calculation involves a series of multiplications and permutations on the digits, resulting in a single checksum digit.
It is important to note the difference between error detection and security. Since the checksum is a single digit (0-9), there is a 0.1 probability that a completely random 12-digit number will accidentally pass the Verhoeff check.
Therefore, the Verhoeff checksum is not a security feature against malicious guessing. Its value lies entirely in ensuring data integrity against human error in real-world data entry scenarios. The un-guessability and security of the Aadhaar number are derived from the massive numeric space provided by the 11 random digits.
The Verhoeff checksum is a silent guardian of data quality in one of the world's most ambitious identity projects.

Today I explored different chunking strategies for RAG systems, and it’s clear that choosing the right approach directly impacts retrieval quality.
Here are the key strategies I understand, along with their trade-offs:
1. Character-based splitting
✔️ Pros:
Simple to implement
Works consistently across all text types
Predictable chunk sizes
❌ Cons:
Breaks context mid-sentence
Poor semantic understanding
Lower retrieval accuracy
2. Recursive character splitting
✔️ Pros:
Preserves structure better than fixed splitting
Flexible fallback mechanism (paragraph → sentence → word)
Balanced chunk quality
❌ Cons:
Still not fully semantic
Requires tuning (chunk size, overlap)
3. Document-specific splitting
✔️ Pros:
Leverages document structure (headings, sections, tables)
Better contextual grouping
Ideal for PDFs, markdown, structured docs
❌ Cons:
Format-dependent
Requires custom parsers per document type
4. Semantic chunking
✔️ Pros:
Context-aware splitting
Higher relevance in retrieval
Improves embedding quality
❌ Cons:
Computationally expensive
Requires embeddings during preprocessing
Slower pipeline
5. Agentic / intelligent chunking
✔️ Pros:
Dynamic and context-driven
Can adapt per use case
Potentially best retrieval performance
❌ Cons:
Complex to design
Higher cost and latency
Harder to debug
One approach I’m currently exploring as well and that is Unstructured.io.
It looks promising for handling multi-format documents (PDF, HTML, DOCX, etc.) and extracting structured elements before chunking.
I am Curious about what chunking strategy has worked best in your RAG pipelines and why ?

Building an LLM - concepts and interview question series
Topic 5 – Quantization: PTQ vs. QAT
When asked, “How do you optimize a 70B parameter model for inference?” many candidates jump to “Quantization.” But very few can explain how we get from FP16 down to INT8 or INT4 without turning the model into gibberish.
The bridge between massive weights and limited GPU VRAM is carefully engineered quantization.
So to control the trade-off between perplexity (accuracy) and latency, you must distinguish between two paradigms:
Post Training Quantization (PTQ) - This is like a quick win. You take a pre-trained Llama-3 or Mistral checkpoint and calibrate it after training.
Mechanics: Uses a small dataset to fix activation ranges.
Used - Flows like load_in_8bit or GGUF fall here. (for more see bitsandbytes package documentation)
Reason it works: Large models have parameter redundancy. They can "eat" the precision loss with <1% perplexity degradation.
Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) – So here you inject fake quantization nodes during a fine-tuning stage. The model “sees” the rounding noise during the forward pass and adapts its weights to survive it. (Fake it till you make it for LLMs :))
Used: Critical for INT4 (and below) or on-device SLMs.
Reason it works: At 4-bit precision, naive PTQ often breaks reasoning capabilities. QAT recovers that lost signal.
Why this matters for LLMs: A 7B model in FP16 needs ~14GB VRAM. In INT8, it needs ~7GB. This isn't just about saving space; it is about Memory Bandwidth. Since inference is memory-bound, moving 50% less data per token directly translates to higher tokens-per-second.
Real-World Example: Meta’s DLRM Recommender systems at scale (like Meta’s) are often bottlenecked by fetching massive Embedding Tables from memory, not by the CPU compute. By applying QAT to these embeddings, they compress Terabyte-scale tables by 4x. This frees up memory bandwidth, allowing the model to run drastically faster in production without losing the nuances about user preferences.
My Rule of Thumb: Start with a solid PTQ process. If perplexity degrades beyond tolerance (especially at 4-bits)—pay the compute cost for QAT.
