    Lots of nonsense is written about the new climate scenarios for IPCC. As an author of the ScenarioMIP paper and former IPCC author, here’s what I think matters. IPCC does *not* create scenarios; in reality, it only assesses them and their climate impacts. ScenarioMIP is a community effort to coordinate CMIP7 simulations for IPCC. It’s important and CMIP7 undeniably frames discussions, but there are thousands of scenarios. The process behind ScenarioMIP is inclusive and rigorous, involving reviews and workshops. It tries to satisfy all needs, but it’s not perfect. The debate is about eliminating the highest scenario RCP8.5 and its misinterpretation that climate is no longer a problem. RCP8.5 scenario was never a “likely” scenario, but built to explore the consequences of high emissions. As one of “baseline scenarios without climate mitigation”, it unfortunately and inappropriately became a “business as usual” scenario.Two excellent summaries linked in the comments. Rather than an attempt to mislead, I think this reflects ignorance of what scenarios are. They are “what if”, a tool to understand the response, to explore drivers and consequences of choices. Scenarios are not predictions and don’t have likelihoods. Scenarios must be broad to explore plausible consequences. Overconfidence is deadly. But what is plausible? Extreme scenarios are often the most interesting ones to explore vulnerability. For nuclear power plants, for tropical storms, we spend most of our time exploring worst cases not because they are likely, but because we want to be prepared for the unlikely. Why did RCP8.5 get prominent with climate models? We are isolating trends from natural variability, and simulations are expensive. Cut emissions by 3 and you need 9 times more simulations (sqrt(N)) for the same signal/noise ratio. At 10K-100K per run, you make choices. If computing is limited, you always learn more from a high emission scenario, then you can scale to lower ones, as IPCC did with the global warming levels. Interpolation is easier than extrapolation. Want to explore model differences and uncertainties? You pick the scenario that models have run first, which is RCP8.5. RCP8.5 has become the de facto standard as a stress test, and for very practical reasons of computing, data availability and a clear climate signal, not to mislead people. Certain outcomes become implausible as we move on. We’ve bent the emission curve, so inevitably the highest “no mitigation” scenarios are gone. This is a "prevention paradox": the worst-case predictions didn't manifest not because we were stupid, but because we took action to stop them. 1.5°C without overshoot can also be ruled out. We are doing better, but we are still not doing enough. Current policies point to ~3°C rather than 1-5°C. The claim that climate change is not a problem is nonsense. So there is progress on mitigation, but not enough. And as long as CO2 emissions remain above zero, the world continues to warm.

    Model governance, including model validation, is a well-practiced discipline. The traditional
    approaches have served the industry well over the years. These typically involve activities
    such as backtesting, performing methodology and documentation reviews, benchmarking
    models, and performing qualitative assessments. A central part of this governance process
    is in model explainability, where knowing the exact variables and their weights is considered
    key. In the emerging world of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning models, it is
    often very difficult to understand model components, and traditional approaches might not
    be ideal. Such new models, known as black box models, require a re-think on how we
    approach model governance. This paper proposes a new framework for model governance
    that does not depend on knowing specific details about what is inside the model. Such an
    approach can enable institutions, particularly regulated financial ones, to be able to use
    black box models with confidence.

    "The book is a comprehensive guide for developing, implementing and monitoring credit risk scorecards." (Institute of Chartered Financial Analysts of India, November 2006)
    Using credit risk scorecards helps large companies manage and minimize their risk exposure and thus improve their bottom lines. Most books on credit scoring emphasize the statistics-related aspects; Credit Risk Scorecards emphasizes the business-related aspects. This unique, business-focused methodology results in more robust scorecard development for real-world, company-wide applications. With this guide to intelligent credit scoring, readers will:

    Have a solid foundation in statistic and data mining principles
    Understand an end-to-end scorecard development and implementation process—from a business perspective
    Know how to incorporate business knowledge to facilitate better scorecard development
    Have practical knowledge of all aspects of scorecard development and implementation, including development roles, model building, validation, implementation, and post-implementation
    Understand how to relate credit risk scoring to Basel II Accord requirements
    Know how to use scorecards to develop risk; adjusted strategies, predict delinquencies, and perform vintage curve analysis to generate highly accurate credit loss forecasts

    Credit Risk Scorecards explains a generic development methodology that is applicable globally. All locations, departments, and lines of business can use the credit risk scorecard; this results in a much more accurate analysis of a company's total exposure than can be derived from reports based on the disparate methodologies of various entities.

    A valuable resource for treasurers, CFOs, credit risk analysts/managers; credit scoring analyst managers; and credit risk executives in banking, retail lending, telecommunications, and insurance; as well as undergraduate and graduate students—this book helps businesses know the score!
