SHAP tells you which features drove a prediction. It does not tell you what the prediction is actually made of.
For retrieval-based models — nearest-neighbor, kernel smoothing and tabular in-context learning — the prediction is a weighted average of training examples. The direct explanation is not a feature-attribution vector. It is a weighted neighborhood: which training rows were consulted, how much weight they received and whether their labels agreed.
The tenth post in my Learning as Geometry Discovery series uses soft k-nearest neighbors as a teaching model to make this distinction precise. Because soft kNN’s weights are mathematically exact and fully visible, we can compare SHAP directly against the true explanation object without estimation ambiguity.
Four demonstrations show where the gap opens. Two queries with nearly identical predictions and nearly identical SHAP values can have completely different local evidence — one supported by a tight coherent neighborhood, the other an accidental average over conflicting labels. A feature SHAP identifies as important may be driving the prediction not through a structural effect but through neighbor switching — changing which training examples are retrieved. A feature with no structural role in the response can still generate large SHAP values by controlling which part of the data manifold the query falls into.
This post is a stepping stone. The next one applies the same framework to TabICL, where neighbor weights are implicit rather than explicit.

I keep hearing people say:
“Manage your agentic AI the way you manage a junior associate.”
I get the appeal. It makes the whole thing feel familiar.
But I think it is the wrong approach.
A junior associate can be coached.
An intern can be supervised.
A new analyst can be pulled aside and told, “Please don’t do that again.”
They usually do not respond by calling seventeen APIs, misreading an outdated policy, creating a customer exception, updating a workflow and confidently emailing Legal.
Agentic AI is not a junior employee.
It moves faster than you.
It has access to more information than you.
It can operate across tools, data, memory and workflows at machine speed.
And when it is wrong, it does not get embarrassed.
It does not pause and think, “Maybe I overstepped.”
It does not develop professional judgment after a tough conversation.
It does not learn humility from a partner’s redline comments.
It just keeps going.
Confidently.
So no, I would not manage agentic AI like an intern.
I would govern it like a very powerful, very fast and occasionally unhinged system with tool access.
That means contracts, authorization, evidence checks, audit trails, runtime controls, fail-closed behavior and continuous monitoring.
The better analogy is not a junior associate.
It is closer to a very smart beast with a Bloomberg terminal, a Python environment, enterprise data access and absolutely no remorse.
You do not give that thing weekly feedback.
You build a harness.

Tabular Foundational Models (e.g., TabPFN and TabICL) are k-nearest neighbors with two upgrades.
Upgrade one: the notion of "similar" is learned, not hand-picked.
Upgrade two: it was learned from thousands of synthetic datasets, not from yours — so deployment is a single forward pass with no fitting.
The ninth post in my Learning as Geometry Discovery series applies the geometric vocabulary built over the previous eight posts to a concrete question: what is a tabular in-context learning model actually doing?
The answer connects three frameworks from the series. From the kernels post: attention is exactly Nadaraya-Watson with a learned affinity, and the scoring matrix M is a geometric object you can inspect directly. From the directional geometry post: decomposing M into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts separates mutual similarity from directional structure — and reveals whether the model's geometry matches the deployment task. From the algebra post: the eigenstructure of the symmetric part is a fingerprint of the pretraining prior, not the data.
The post includes figures and code from the companion notebook, including the noise-dimension experiment (fixed kernels collapse, learned attention adapts), the Bayes posterior predictive comparison (the trained model closely approximates the Bayesian optimum) and the M = S + A decomposition heatmaps.

Most agent test failures aren't failures of the model. They're failures of coverage.
A team writes fifty test prompts. The agent passes forty-seven. They ship. Three days later, production failures start because real users phrase questions in ways no expert imagined.
My latest essay makes a single reframe that changes everything:
If a document is the source of truth, the same document can generate the questions and grade the answers automatically, deterministically, without a human in the loop: No hand-written prompts. No LLM-as-judge.
The key is a DocumentGraph, a knowledge graph automatically constructed from the source document's structure. Tables become typed triples. Bullet lists become key-value facts. Every numeric value is stored exactly with integrity hashing. The graph is the ground truth, by construction.
From there:
→ 13 question categories are generated by walking the graph from simple fact completion to multi-hop reasoning, contradiction detection and policy compliance
→ Presentation becomes a structured factor space (clarity, entity aliasing, adversarial framing, role, tool availability and more) sampled with Design of Experiments and not random calls
→ Every answer is graded by the same graph that generated the question deterministically with a full audit trail back to the source document
The result: continuous regression testing, audit-grade traceability and corpus-level scaling without writing a single test prompt.

Correlation is symmetric. Causality is not.
Distance, kernel similarity, co-membership in a partition cell — all the geometric frameworks covered so far in this series share one property: they are symmetric. If x is close to z, then z is close to x. That symmetry is a source of mathematical power. It is also a constraint.
Many of the structures that matter most in machine learning are not symmetric. A cause precedes its effect. Token i attending to token j is not the same as token j attending to token i. In a directed graph, a path from x to z does not imply a path from z to x. In a supply chain, reversing the flow produces a different system.
The eighth post in my Learning as Geometry Discovery series introduces directional geometry — the tools for structures where order, flow and asymmetry carry essential information. It covers directed graphs as geometry of reachability and influence, the R = S + A decomposition that separates mutual from directional structure, trajectories and flow fields as directed dynamics through state space, asymmetric distances and quasimetrics, causality as directed acyclic structure, self-attention as a learned directional relation and noncommutativity as a geometric fact rather than an algebraic technicality.
Symmetric tools see what is mutual. Directional tools see what goes which way. Both are needed.

Governed agentic AI is not just a policy question.
It is a runtime question.
I’m looking forward to joining Sri Krishnamurthy, CFA for Session 2 of QuantUniversity AI Spring School 2026, where we will discuss risk, controls and operating readiness for agentic AI in financial services.
My focus will be on runtime governance: how to build a harness around agentic AI systems so governance is not an after-the-fact review, but part of how the system actually operates.
For agentic AI, the control problem changes.
We are no longer validating a single model output. We are governing a system that can reason, retrieve, call tools, update memory, follow policies, take actions and change behavior across turns.
That means governance must move closer to execution.
A runtime harness should help answer questions such as:
Can the agent do this?
Is the user authorized?
Is the tool allowed?
Is the retrieved evidence sufficient?
Did the agent follow policy?
Was the decision trace captured?
Can we replay what happened?
Can we stop, escalate or constrain the action before harm occurs?
In banking and financial services, this is the difference between experimenting with agentic AI and deploying it responsibly.
The future of governed agentic AI will not be built with prompts alone.
It will require architecture, controls, telemetry, verification, testing and evidence.
Looking forward to the discussion.

𝐂𝐚𝐫𝐭 𝐁𝐞𝐟𝐨𝐫𝐞 𝐓𝐡𝐞 𝐇𝐨𝐫𝐬𝐞 (𝐏𝐨𝐬𝐭 𝟐/𝟑 – 𝐄𝐧𝐭𝐞𝐫𝐩𝐫𝐢𝐬𝐞 𝐀𝐈 𝐈𝐧𝐬𝐢𝐠𝐡𝐭)
The core issue is not that enterprises lack AI governance. The issue is that governance is not the same as financial risk pricing.
Traditional model risk management helps organizations document, validate, test, approve, monitor, and govern models. Those disciplines matter. But validation does not automatically tell a CFO, Treasurer, Chief Risk Officer, Audit Committee, or Board what the enterprise stands to lose when an AI system fails.
Model risk management asks: 𝐈𝐬 𝐭𝐡𝐞 𝐦𝐨𝐝𝐞𝐥 𝐟𝐢𝐭 𝐟𝐨𝐫 𝐮𝐬𝐞?
AI risk pricing asks: 𝐖𝐡𝐚𝐭 𝐝𝐨𝐞𝐬 𝐭𝐡𝐞 𝐞𝐧𝐭𝐞𝐫𝐩𝐫𝐢𝐬𝐞 𝐬𝐭𝐚𝐧𝐝 𝐭𝐨 𝐥𝐨𝐬𝐞 𝐰𝐡𝐞𝐧 𝐭𝐡𝐞 𝐦𝐨𝐝𝐞𝐥 𝐢𝐬 𝐰𝐫𝐨𝐧𝐠, 𝐦𝐢𝐬𝐮𝐬𝐞𝐝, 𝐨𝐯𝐞𝐫-𝐭𝐫𝐮𝐬𝐭𝐞𝐝, 𝐥𝐞𝐠𝐚𝐥𝐥𝐲 𝐜𝐡𝐚𝐥𝐥𝐞𝐧𝐠𝐞𝐝, 𝐨𝐫 𝐞𝐦𝐛𝐞𝐝𝐝𝐞𝐝 𝐢𝐧𝐭𝐨 𝐚 𝐜𝐫𝐢𝐭𝐢𝐜𝐚𝐥 𝐛𝐮𝐬𝐢𝐧𝐞𝐬𝐬 𝐩𝐫𝐨𝐜𝐞𝐬𝐬?
That distinction matters.
NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework[1] is an important step because it helps organizations govern, map, measure, and manage AI risk. But it is not a pricing system. It does not provide enterprises with a path to a market-cleared premium, reserve factor, loss curve, deductible, coverage limit, or benchmarked financial exposure for a specific AI use case.
This is where enterprise AI adoption is out of sequence. A CFO would not approve a major acquisition, credit exposure, hedging strategy, plant expansion, or cyber program simply because a governance checklist exists. The business case must connect expected return to downside exposure.
AI should be no different. Yet many AI business cases are still built around productivity, automation, speed, and competitive necessity while the downside risk remains financially absent.
Meaning, many enterprises are not truly pricing AI investment risk. They are funding upside assumptions and hoping governance catches the downside.
That is not mature capital allocation. That is confidence without a price.

Last week I had the good fortune to present on the increasingly relevant topic of AI Governance at the 28th #GFMI Model Risk Conference in Boston. My discussion focused on model inventories, model transparency and observability (as it relates specifically to AI), and the evolving #MRM regulatory landscape, with a focus on the implications of the EU AI Act, the challenge of defining and implementing “responsible AI,” and the growing need for all firms (not just financial institutions) to identify where AI actually resides — whether embedded in vendor software, internally developed tools, or agentic systems. With the advent of AI, a robust model inventory has become all the more indispensable.
I also highlighted the rapid growth of tooling that supports AI observability, traceability, and monitoring. As AI become more ubiquitous (and more autonomous), governance will increasingly depend on monitoring performance outcomes and ensuring that adequate controls are in place, in a way that is a well-established MRM discipline.
My talk followed that of David Palmer from the Federal Reserve, whose presentation parsed updates to supervisory guidance introduced last month through SR 26-2. Later, Rama Subramanian gave an thoughtful presentation on “industrializing” the Model Risk Management function — an area of growing importance as governance demands continue to scale that is of interest to me and my work. Amit Srivastav followed with a discussion of how Internal Audit is evolving to the age of agentic AI. Finally, Carlos Rodriguez 🇵🇷 closed the event with his insights on how best to incorporate the oversight of AI within existing MRM frameworks.
After the event, I was also fortunate enough to participate in a roundtable on the topic of agentic led by Sri Krishnamurthy, CFA and Agus Sudjianto, alongside many other distinguished colleagues across the industry, including Rodanthy Tzani, Simone Flory, Chris Smigielski, Ritu Sharma, and Joseph St Charles. Thanks to MassChallenge and John Valentine for being such great hosts.
A special thanks to Stephen Boras for coordinating such a well-run and timely event, as well as to Giovanna Lollo for including me again this year - and for putting together the event. Look forward to the next installment in NYC later this year!

𝐂𝐚𝐫𝐭 𝐁𝐞𝐟𝐨𝐫𝐞 𝐓𝐡𝐞 𝐇𝐨𝐫𝐬𝐞 (𝐏𝐨𝐬𝐭 𝟏/𝟑 – 𝐄𝐧𝐭𝐞𝐫𝐩𝐫𝐢𝐬𝐞 𝐀𝐈 𝐏𝐫𝐨𝐛𝐥𝐞𝐦)
Most major enterprise technologies mature alongside financial methods for pricing, transferring, benchmarking, and accruing/reserving against risk. 𝐀𝐈 𝐢𝐬 𝐩𝐫𝐨𝐠𝐫𝐞𝐬𝐬𝐢𝐧𝐠 𝐝𝐢𝐟𝐟𝐞𝐫𝐞𝐧𝐭𝐥𝐲.
𝐂𝐲𝐛𝐞𝐫 𝐫𝐢𝐬𝐤 did not remain a purely technical concern. It became an insurance market, with premiums, limits, exclusions, loss histories, and underwriting benchmarks. Munich Re[1] expects the global cyber insurance market to reach $16.3 billion in 2025, while NAIC[2] reported nearly $15 billion in global cyber premium written in 2024.

Building a multi-agent pipeline that reads a 144-page regulation and answers compliance questions is no longer hard.
- RAG handles retrieval.
- Context windows keep growing — 128K tokens is standard, 1M is available.
- Guardrails catch toxic outputs and prompt injections.
- LangGraph, CrewAI, AutoGen — the orchestration frameworks are mature.
The part that's still unsolved: how do you know the answer is actually grounded in the document?
I ran an experiment. Three agents powered by GPT-5.4-mini over the EU AI Act (https://lnkd.in/eGY-N2y7):
→ 🤖 Retriever searches the full regulation text
→ 🤖 Compliance analyst answers from the retrieved passages
→ 🤖 Risk assessor translates findings into business impact
Five real questions. The agents produced fluent, confident, well-structured answers to all five.
🔴 Two of them were wrong.
On "what fines apply to prohibited AI practices," the retriever pulled passages about technical standards instead of Article 99. The analyst honestly said the text didn't cover fines. But a less conservative model would have filled the gap from training data — and you'd never know.
Then I ran a sabotage test: deliberately fed the wrong regulation section to the analyst. Both metrics flagged it independently — retrieval relevance caught the topic mismatch, SGI caught the model's inability to ground its response in irrelevant text. Two signals, same conclusion.
This is what we built groundlens to detect. No second LLM call, no probabilistic judge — geometric distance between the response and the source material. Deterministic. The score tells you: trust this, review this, or flag this.

Single-method retrieval often fails in #financial and #regulatory domains, exact-term search misses semantic relationships, while dense models overlook structured identifiers. A multi-path approach resolves this gap.
The Multi-Path Retrieval (#MPR) pipeline implements four complementary retrieval strategies with a domain-specialized re-ranking mechanism to balance lexical precision and semantic understanding:
#BM25 Sparse Retriever – captures exact matches for regulatory codes, ticker symbols, and key phrases.
#Dense Semantic Retriever – identifies paraphrases and conceptually similar expressions through high-dimensional embeddings.
#Metadata-Aware Retriever – filters at section-level granularity, narrowing retrieval to relevant document parts before detailed chunk search.
#HyDE Retriever – generates a hypothetical answer, embeds it, and retrieves real text segments with similar structure and terminology, bridging the question–answer linguistic gap.
Final results can be aggregated through weighted rank fusion (like semantic > BM25 > metadata > HyDE ), producing top-ranked chunks enriched through context bundling when adjacent similarities exceed 0.85. This ensures coherent, context-complete passages are provided to downstream models.
Such architecture is especially effective in financial compliance and regulatory analysis, where both formal precision and contextual interpretation are essential. The hybrid fusion enhances #recall, #contextualaccuracy, and #interpretability in retrieval-augmented systems(#RAG).

Runtime authorization is the unsolved problem in AI governance.
Wrapping probabilistic models in policies, review boards, and dashboards guarantees failure between validation checkpoints, as shown in every major LLM agent incident to date. Governance built around a system cannot answer the critical operational question: What mechanism authorizes the engine to execute at the exact moment of execution?
The answer cannot be a policy document. It must be the execution substrate itself.
The substrate requires a valid execution envelope for every representable action—a time-bounded mathematical contract established at load-time:
(authority_id · stop_right_holder · liability_binding · t_issue · t_expiry)
The stop_right_holder names the agent with unilateral termination authority. The liability_binding attaches a verifiable legal instrument to the execution itself, rather than to a log entry produced afterward.
When the current timestamp exceeds t_expiry, the architecture blocks the action and triggers an alert. It renders execution structurally unrepresentable. That distinction separates a fragile guardrail from an invariant substrate.
Three mechanisms make this boundary non-bypassable:
1. Reset integrity. After a REFUSE state, the runtime snapshot compares the current operational scope against the last verified state. Any scope increase demands a new envelope and explicit steward re-authorization. Systems cannot reset into elevated privileges.
2. Diagnostic boundaries. The architecture restricts BORDERLINE states to diagnostic functions only. Probabilistic optimizers cannot target the safety boundary as a performance strategy because the architecture makes that state non-targetable by design.
3. Cryptographic evidence. The infrastructure hash-chains every commitment into an append-only ledger at the exact moment of execution, eliminating post-hoc reconstruction. The unalterable record is the boundary, not a trail leading back to it.
Most enterprise stacks combine policy files with logging and label the result as governance.
That architecture has a structural vulnerability: unauthorized execution remains mathematically representable at runtime. The system could act outside its authorized scope. It relies on hope and auditors catching what matters after execution completes.
A genuinely governed runtime eliminates that reliance. Unauthorized execution becomes impossible by construction, not by convention.
The boundary does not surround the system. It is the system.
