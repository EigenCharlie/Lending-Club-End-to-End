Lots of nonsense is written about the new climate scenarios for IPCC. As an author of the ScenarioMIP paper and former IPCC author, here’s what I think matters.
IPCC does *not* create scenarios; in reality, it only assesses them and their climate impacts.
ScenarioMIP is a community effort to coordinate CMIP7 simulations for IPCC. It’s important and CMIP7 undeniably frames discussions, but there are thousands of scenarios. The process behind ScenarioMIP is inclusive and rigorous, involving reviews and workshops. It tries to satisfy all needs, but it’s not perfect.
The debate is about eliminating the highest scenario RCP8.5 and its misinterpretation that climate is no longer a problem. RCP8.5 scenario was never a “likely” scenario, but built to explore the consequences of high emissions. As one of “baseline scenarios without climate mitigation”, it unfortunately and inappropriately became a “business as usual” scenario.Two excellent summaries linked in the comments.
Rather than an attempt to mislead, I think this reflects ignorance of what scenarios are. They are “what if”, a tool to understand the response, to explore drivers and consequences of choices. Scenarios are not predictions and don’t have likelihoods.
Scenarios must be broad to explore plausible consequences. Overconfidence is deadly. But what is plausible? Extreme scenarios are often the most interesting ones to explore vulnerability. For nuclear power plants, for tropical storms, we spend most of our time exploring worst cases not because they are likely, but because we want to be prepared for the unlikely.
Why did RCP8.5 get prominent with climate models? We are isolating trends from natural variability, and simulations are expensive. Cut emissions by 3 and you need 9 times more simulations (sqrt(N)) for the same signal/noise ratio. At 10K-100K per run, you make choices. If computing is limited, you always learn more from a high emission scenario, then you can scale to lower ones, as IPCC did with the global warming levels. Interpolation is easier than extrapolation. Want to explore model differences and uncertainties? You pick the scenario that models have run first, which is RCP8.5. RCP8.5 has become the de facto standard as a stress test, and for very practical reasons of computing, data availability and a clear climate signal, not to mislead people.
Certain outcomes become implausible as we move on. We’ve bent the emission curve, so inevitably the highest “no mitigation” scenarios are gone. This is a "prevention paradox": the worst-case predictions didn't manifest not because we were stupid, but because we took action to stop them.
1.5°C without overshoot can also be ruled out. We are doing better, but we are still not doing enough. Current policies point to ~3°C rather than 1-5°C. The claim that climate change is not a problem is nonsense.
So there is progress on mitigation, but not enough. And as long as CO2 emissions remain above zero, the world continues to warm.

Networking is...
building a web of relationships with others for mutual support, growth and learning.
Networking is not...
transactional. It's not about collecting names and numbers.
Approaching networking with the right mindset, one focused on giving before asking, can make all the difference in the world.
Here are two suggestions for adopting a healthy mindset around networking.
1️⃣ Be confident that you have something to offer.
Realize that you have much to *give* to others based on your life experiences. Even if you are not an expert in your field, you have knowledge that someone else wants or needs to receive. But if you don't share it, they won't know.
Asking questions when you first meet people is a great strategy for identifying the people that you can help - and that might be able to help you.
2️⃣Know that others are nervous too.
Even the most experienced networkers get butterflies walking into a room of new people.
Networking is about building and cultivating relationships. No matter what industry you're in, you need other people to help you succeed.
Whether you want to talk with people across the street or across the world, you will likely connect first through digital communication.
Subsequent interactions will likely be a combination of online and offline as you build relationships.
👉 Enter a social platform like LinkedIn, that can help you integrate your online and offline engagement.
Ever thought about LinkedIn as a tool to help you network and make more meaningful connections?
You might want to.
💫 Embrace a *giving* mindset and think about not just what you might be able to gain from knowing other people, but also what can you give that may help someone else.

Model governance, including model validation, is a well-practiced discipline. The traditional
approaches have served the industry well over the years. These typically involve activities
such as backtesting, performing methodology and documentation reviews, benchmarking
models, and performing qualitative assessments. A central part of this governance process
is in model explainability, where knowing the exact variables and their weights is considered
key. In the emerging world of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning models, it is
often very difficult to understand model components, and traditional approaches might not
be ideal. Such new models, known as black box models, require a re-think on how we
approach model governance. This paper proposes a new framework for model governance
that does not depend on knowing specific details about what is inside the model. Such an
approach can enable institutions, particularly regulated financial ones, to be able to use
black box models with confidence.

Using credit risk scorecards helps large companies manage and minimize their risk exposure and thus improve their bottom lines. Most books on credit scoring emphasize the statistics-related aspects; Credit Risk Scorecards emphasizes the business-related aspects. This unique, business-focused methodology results in more robust scorecard development for real-world, company-wide applications. With this guide to intelligent credit scoring, readers will:
Have a solid foundation in statistic and data mining principles
Understand an end-to-end scorecard development and implementation process—from a business perspective
Know how to incorporate business knowledge to facilitate better scorecard development
Have practical knowledge of all aspects of scorecard development and implementation, including development roles, model building, validation, implementation, and post-implementation
Understand how to relate credit risk scoring to Basel II Accord requirements
Know how to use scorecards to develop risk; adjusted strategies, predict delinquencies, and perform vintage curve analysis to generate highly accurate credit loss forecasts
Credit Risk Scorecards explains a generic development methodology that is applicable globally. All locations, departments, and lines of business can use the credit risk scorecard; this results in a much more accurate analysis of a company's total exposure than can be derived from reports based on the disparate methodologies of various entities.
A valuable resource for treasurers, CFOs, credit risk analysts/managers; credit scoring analyst managers; and credit risk executives in banking, retail lending, telecommunications, and insurance; as well as undergraduate and graduate students—this book helps businesses know the score!

Credit Scoring for SAS ®Enterprise Miner™ has been widely used to develop binary target probability of default scorecards, which include scorecards for application and behavior scoring. Extending the weight -of-evidence binned scorecard methodology to interval - based targets such
as loss given default and exposure at default is a natural step in the evolution of scorecard development methodologies.
This paper discusses several new methods available in Credit Scoring for SAS Enterprise Miner that help build scorecards that are based on interval targets. These include cutoffs that convert the interval target to a binary form and an algorithm that maintains the continuous nature of the
interval target.
Each method is covered in detail, and all methods are compared to determine how they perform
against known target values.

I am currently reading Mark Carney’s book – Value(s). It is a comprehensive and interesting look at banking around the world including the beginnings of the global monetary system and the gold standard. Canada’s banking system is globally recognized for its stability, conservative regulation, and resilience, especially during crises like the 2008 financial meltdown. I feel quite strongly that you cannot determine a path forward without a strong understanding of where you have been and where you are now. This chapter outlines the structure and key players in the current system, including the Big Five banks, credit unions, and the Bank of Canada.
Canada’s financial core is dominated by five major banks:
Bank Distinctive Feature
RBC Largest by market cap; global reach
TD Strong U.S. retail presence
Scotiabank Focus on Latin America
BMO Canada’s oldest bank
CIBC Domestic retail and small business focus
Key Traits:
· Regulated by Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions
· Strong capital and liquidity
· Offer full-service financial products
· Emphasize long-term stability over short-term risk
Credit unions offer a community-based alternative to big banks:
· Democratic governance: One member, one vote
· Provincial regulation
· Personalized service and lower fees
They are especially important in rural and underserved areas and often lead in tech adoption.
The Bank of Canada ensures monetary and financial stability through:
· Monetary policy (interest rate setting)
· Currency issuance
· Systemic risk monitoring
· Innovation research (e.g., CBDCs)
It targets 2% inflation and acts decisively during crises, such as using quantitative easing during COVID-19.
Canada’s system is praised for being “boring”—a strength rooted in:
· Strict regulation
· High capital buffers
· Minimal subprime exposure
· Deposit insurance (CDIC)
· Strong bank-regulator collaboration
This framework supports cautious innovation, including fintech integration.
Canada’s banking model blends scale and security (Big Five), community responsiveness (credit unions), and monetary discipline (Bank of Canada). It offers a compelling case study in how to balance innovation with systemic safety—a theme explored further in the next chapter on fintech.
