Datasets are dominated by "good" cases, so practitioners often turn to resampling techniques: for example, creating a 50/50 split of goods and bads to use accuracy and related metrics. The challenge is that a model trained on such a balanced sample won't reflect the observed risk levels. Model predictions will be miscalibrated and, without post-hoc correction, they can't be trusted as probabilities of default. The deck shows what happens when you train on a 50/50 dataset and then move back to the real-world distribution. I compare several techniques across different models to highlight when they succeed (and when they don't) at recovering calibrated default/fraud probabilities. PhD in ML | Generative AI & Data Centric AI 9mo When I was developing large fraud models at HSBC, calibration was absolutely a standard practice. Rebalancing the dataset for the training it OK, but for validation and test, we should ALWAYS use the natural distribution that the model will encounter in the field. But this is not enough, usually there are conflicting and imbalanced objectives that we have to account for, e.g. a risk tolerance of 2% on FN versus an expected 70% gain on TN in case of adjudication models. In this case, building a single model does not give the full picture, even if well calibrated.
