The integration of AI into modern loan underwriting is set to reshape how institutions approach credit decisioning. As borrower profiles evolve and regulatory expectations grow, traditional scoring models, including those based on transactional mining patterns, remain essential, but often fall short when addressing edge cases and adapting in real time.
With the advent of large language models (LLMs), financial institutions can now deploy multi-agent AI systems, where each agent specializes in a specific task. One agent might pull key financial details from applications, another might check whether decisions follow policy, and a third might finalize the recommendation. Throughout this process, a human underwriter provides human-in-the-loop oversight.
Not only does this collaborative framework accelerate decision-making but it also improves consistency, transparency, and adaptability across the underwriting pipeline as illustrated in the diagram below.
Simplified decision tree used by AI Underwriters to assess applications based on risk thresholds.
So what is an AI Underwriter? At its core, it’s an assistant that learns decision patterns from past loan data and applies lending policies to help evaluate new applicants. Like human underwriters, these agents benefit from structured onboarding and continuous exposure to new cases.
AI Underwriter Assistants
Before AI underwriting systems can operate effectively, they must be trained just like human underwriters. Human expertise develops through reviewing past cases and gaining hands-on experience before making independent decisions. Similarly, AI-based underwriting assistants require structured onboarding, typically delivered through prompt-based instructions, after which they begin to learn decision-making patterns from historical data aligned with real-world lending policies. Over time, these agents can refine their logic, improve accuracy, and operate within established compliance frameworks.
OpenAI's Assistants API allows users to create AI-powered underwriting systems capable of handling multiple risk assessment tasks simultaneously. Previously, coordinating multiple OpenAI Assistants to collaborate on a task was difficult. However, last years' improvements in the Assistants API, along with the introduction of the Agents SDK, have opened new possibilities for building more modular, intelligent, and context-aware systems.
In our workflow, we leverage the Assistants API specifically for nuanced, narrative-driven analysis, where each assistant brings domain-specific reasoning.
In this example, we use two AI underwriting assistants:
Underwriter – Analyzes past loan applications and prepares key financial insights.
Decision Maker – Uses these insights, along with credit scores from a scoring model, to make final approval decisions.
To train and evaluate these assistants, we use two datasets:
Training Set (700 records with credit history)
Test Set (100 records without credit history) + a model-generated credit score

The interaction between Underwriter and Decision Maker is structured as follows:
The underwriter introduces themselves and summarizes their role. The decision-maker introduces themselves and clarifies their expectations.
The underwriter highlights key insights from the data analysis. The decision-maker uses the insights from the underwriting report, analyzes credit scores, and makes final decisions.

Overall, the system shows strong promise as a reliable decision-support tool in real-world underwriting scenarios.

Concluding remarks
As financial institutions navigate increasing complexity in credit decisioning, AI-enabled, human-in-the-loop underwriting frameworks are emerging as a promising way forward. With borrower profiles evolving and regulatory scrutiny rising, traditional and transaction-based scoring models remain instrumental but often fall short in handling edge cases or adapting to trends. By embedding large language models into multi-agent workflows, institutions can automate routine risk assessments while preserving expert judgment where it matters most.
