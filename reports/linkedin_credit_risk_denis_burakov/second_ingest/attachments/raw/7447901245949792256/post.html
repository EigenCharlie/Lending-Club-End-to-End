You already understand LLMs better than you think. NLP and credit risk seem like different worlds, but the statistical foundation is identical. MLE connects your scorecard's logistic regression to a spaCy word counter to GPT's training loop. Most data scientists in credit risk do not work extensively with text. The only applications I have seen firsthand were using NLP to extract news for semantic scoring on corporate loans, and web crawlers for alternative data. But the connection runs deeper than applications. A SpaCy word-frequency summarizer estimates 𝖯(𝗐𝗈𝗋𝖽 | 𝖽𝗈𝖼𝗎𝗆𝖾𝗇𝗍) by counting and normalizing. A bigram model does the same for word pairs. GPT does it for entire sequences with billions of parameters. Your scorecard does the same with WOE and logistic regression, just with a different goal. I wrote about this connection with working Python code, walking the full arc from extractive summarization to large language models.     Day 25/30: NLP: Word2Vec Today I worked on Word2Vec, a model that learns word embeddings from context. Instead of predicting a label directly, Word2Vec learns how words relate to each other by turning them into vectors. Words that appear in similar contexts end up closer together in embedding space. For this one, I used a real technical article instead of a toy corpus: the LiteLLM security update from March 2026. I wanted to see how Word2Vec would organize security-related terms from an actual engineering write-up. I used the skip-gram setup, which learns by predicting surrounding words from a given word. What I liked most is that this made embeddings feel much more real. After training the model, I visualized selected words with PCA, and it was interesting to see terms like malicious, payload, pypi, and compromised appear in a similar region, while words like security, incident, investigation, and release landed in other nearby parts of the space. It is not a perfect semantic map, especially because this was trained on a single article, but it still shows how Word2Vec starts building relationships from co-occurrence and context. My biggest takeaway from today is that Word2Vec is one of those foundational NLP ideas that makes later language models easier to appreciate. Before transformers and contextual embeddings, this was already teaching machines that words can have meaning in vector form. One limitation is that Word2Vec learns static embeddings, so a word gets the same vector no matter where it appears.     Day 28/30: Transformers and LLMs: Sentence-BERT Today I worked on Sentence-BERT, which is a more practical version of BERT for sentence embeddings. Yesterday’s BERT example helped me understand contextual embeddings, but today’s model made that idea much more usable. Instead of pulling raw transformer outputs and figuring out sentence similarity from them, Sentence-BERT is built to generate sentence-level embeddings that can be compared efficiently. For this demo, I encoded 8 sentences and compared them using cosine similarity. The similarity heatmap made the difference very clear. Some sentence pairs had much stronger relationships than others: S1 and S2 = 0.43 Both use the word bank, but in different contexts S5 and S6 = 0.56 Both are about language and NLP S6 and S7 = 0.42 Both relate to semantic understanding and embeddings That was the biggest thing that clicked for me today. Sentence-BERT is not just about encoding text. It is about creating embeddings that are actually useful for semantic similarity, search, clustering, and retrieval. Compared to vanilla BERT, this feels much more practical for real-world AI engineering tasks like semantic search and RAG pipelines. My biggest takeaway from today is that Sentence-BERT turns contextual embeddings into something much easier to use for comparing whole sentences. One limitation is that even though it is much more practical than vanilla BERT for similarity tasks, the quality still depends a lot on the pretrained model and the domain of the text.
