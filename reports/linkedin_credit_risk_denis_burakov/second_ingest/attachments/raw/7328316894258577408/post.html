In applied risk problems, most analyses rely on logistic regression to model proportions, such as default or fraud rates. An important point regarding the **business context** in which a model is applied: A **Poisson model** is appropriate when the goal is to estimate the **expected number of events** — such as in insurance, where a single policy may result in multiple claims over its lifetime. In contrast, **logistic regression** is typically used in credit risk modeling to estimate the **probability of a single event**, such as default. These models treat risk as binary — default or no default — since, conceptually, there’s "no resurrection": a default is a terminal event. Even if further activity occurs post-default, it’s usually handled by separate models (e.g., LGD or EAD), not by repeating the original probability model. That’s why aligning the model structure with the **underlying business problem** — not just the data — is crucial.

I agree with Gabriel Ryan, FRM on Poisson and NB. They are of course discrete distributions, so that has issues of its own eg the use of a pmf, not a pdf. In theory, one could fit to a gamma distribution, the continuous extension of these; it is closer to NB in that the mean and variance are unequal. But the larger problem I have seen with counts data is the apparent over representation of instances of zero counts. That is, more than eg Poisson would predict. This is an inherent issue in discrete distributions. Rejecting some or all of the zeroes would bias the data, and so is not recommended. Instead, use a zero-inflated fit. https://stats.oarc.ucla.edu/r/dae/zip/

    🌳 When many models think better than one We often search for “the best model.” But in practice, several average decision trees—voting together—beat a single “genius” tree. That’s the core of a Random Forest: diversity + consensus → stability. Why it works: each tree makes different mistakes. When you bag samples and limit features, errors cancel out, variance drops, and predictions become more reliable. For classification, they vote; for regression, they average. Simple idea, huge impact. If you’re starting out: try n_estimators=100, cap max_depth, validate with a holdout set, and track accuracy/precision/recall. Then tune. You’ll see why Random Forests are a default baseline in real projects. Don’t chase perfection in one model—design a team of models that generalize better together.
