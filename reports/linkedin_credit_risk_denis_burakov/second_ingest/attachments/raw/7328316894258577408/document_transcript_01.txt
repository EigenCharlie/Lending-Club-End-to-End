Poisson Models 1 1 Introduction Poisson distribution, introduced by Siméon Denis Poisson in 1837, describes the incidence of events during a fixed time interval. Originally developed to model rare, discrete events, this distribution has found widespread applications across diverse fields ranging from estimating the number of customer service calls to modeling insurance claims, network traffic accidents, and other rare events. While this distribution rarely fits real-world data perfectly as it relies on some very strong assumptions, it presents an interesting opportunity to model certain processes that fit within the Poisson framework. Poisson-distributed data can be modeled using parametric linear models, Bayesian inference, or machine learning algorithms such as gradient-boosted trees with a Poisson objective. This text focuses on the linear and ML models and gives several examples of how to apply them in Python. We also provide a Python implementation of the Poisson generalized linear model (GLM) using the Fisher scoring algorithm. Poisson Models 2 • k is the number of occurrences • λ is the expected number of occurrences (rate parameter) Key Properties: • The mean and variance of the Poisson distribution are both equal to λ. • Suitable for modeling rare events over a fixed period or space. • As λ increases, the Poisson distribution approaches a normal distribution. • The probability of zero occurrences (P (X = 0) = e−λ) is particularly important in many applications. Poisson distribution focuses on the incidence rate of rare event counts in isolation from total possible events, un- like proportions (risk or prevalence) modeling commonly performed with logistic regression that uses Bernoulli likelihood that does require the total number of events. When exponentiated, Poisson regression coefficients may be interpreted as incidence rate ratios. The output of the model is a distribution of E[Y|X] (expected value of Y given X) that is Poisson distributed, so for a given observation i, the prediction of Poisson regression does not represent a predicted count, but an expected mean number of events for a given set of input features.1 Given that variance and mean equality is a very strong assumption, Poisson models often fail to fit the real-world data, but there are examples when they do. The 3D visualization above represents the Poisson Probability Mass Function (PMF) with: • λ (Rate) on the horizontal axis: the expected value (mean) of the distribution. • k (Counts) on the vertical axis: the number of events observed. • PMF value on the z-axis: the probability of observing exactly k events given a Poisson process with rate λ. The chart shows how probability changes across different values of λ and k. For small λ values (1-2), probabilities peak at lower k values (0-1). As λ increases (toward 10), the probability mass shifts right, with peaks occurring at higher k values. The color gradient from dark blue to yellow represents increasing probability values. 3 Horse Kick Data: Classic Rare Events Example Deaths from horse kicks might seem like extraordinarily rare events, but for those serving in the Prussian cavalry during the 19th century it was not so uncommon. What makes the case of Prussian horse kicks particularly valuable to statisticians like Sir Ronald Aylmer Fisher is how it can almost perfectly illustrate the Poisson distribution. In Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925), Fisher used Ladislaus Bortkewitch’s Prussian Horse Kicks dataset to introduce Poisson series. The dataset used by Fisher contains incidence of horse-kick accidents for twenty successive years spanning between 1875 and 1894. Notably, both L. Bortkewitsch and R.A. Fisher used only a subset of data (200 records out of 280), since corps denoted G, I, VI, and XI deviated significantly from the average. What Fisher notes is that, with its single parameter λ = 0.61 (the average death rate), the Poisson distribution predicted almost exactly these same numbers. The distribution expected 108.7 instances of zero deaths, 66.3 instances of one death, and so on. 1In many applications such as epidemiology, an offset term is included to account for exposure time or space (e.g., forest density, population size). The German health registry sample included 3,874 individuals and recorded: • 43% of people (1,611) had zero doctor visits • 23% had one or two visits • 18% had three to five visits • 16% had six or more visits Unlike the horse kick data, this everyday counting scenario proved troublesome for the standard Poisson model, which predicted: • Only 7.6% of people would have zero visits (severely underpredicting) • 36.6% would have one or two visits (overpredicting) • 43% would have three to five visits (greatly overpredicting) • 12.8% would have six or more visits (underpredicting) 4.1 Why Excess Zeros Are a Problem When the number of observed zeros exceeds what the Poisson distribution predicts, we have zero inflation. This creates problems because: • Variance > Mean (violates Poisson assumptions) • Overdispersion inflates standard errors and reduces model accuracy • May reflect a two-process model: some units never experience events (structural zeros) A commonly accepted alternative is to use Negative Binomial model for this type of problems. The Negative Binomial distribution adds an extra parameter (dispersion parameter) that allows variance to exceed the mean, making it much more flexible for overdispersed data. This explains why it performs so much better than the standard Poisson model for the doctor visits data.
