    Part 2: The Math of Layer Laziness (Cosine Similarity) 🧮✂️ We’ve wiretapped the neural network. Now, how do we prove a layer is useless? In Part 1, I broke down how to use PyTorch hooks to capture the intermediate hidden states of an LLM during a forward pass. Now we have gigabytes of raw tensor data. To build an ultra-efficient Small Language Model (SLM), we need to mathematically score how much "work" each Transformer block is doing. We do this by calculating the Cosine Similarity between the tensor going into the block and the tensor coming out of it. Here is the underlying math we execute in PyTorch: Cosine Similarity = (A . B) / (||A|| * ||B||) Where $\mathbf{A}$ is the input hidden state and $\mathbf{B}$ is the output hidden state of the block. The Workhorse Layers (Low Similarity): If the cosine similarity is low (e.g., 0.70), it means the vectors are pointing in different directions in the high-dimensional space. The Transformer block actively transformed the data. This layer is critical for reasoning. Keep it. The Lazy Layers (High Similarity): If the cosine similarity is exceptionally high (e.g., 0.99), the input and output vectors are nearly identical. The block applied attention and feed-forward calculations, burned VRAM, consumed compute latency—and ultimately changed almost nothing. When you map these similarity scores across all layers, you uncover the structural redundancy hidden inside these massive open-weight models.     Today, I revisited the concept of the dot product of vectors and realized how a fundamental idea from linear algebra forms the backbone of modern Data Science and Machine Learning. At its core, the dot product is defined algebraically as A · B = ∑(aᵢbᵢ), where corresponding components of two vectors are multiplied and summed to produce a scalar. While this definition is simple, its deeper meaning becomes clear through its geometric interpretation: A · B = |A||B| cos(θ). This equation reveals that the dot product captures not just magnitude, but also the angle between vectors, essentially encoding similarity. In the context of Machine Learning, this interpretation becomes extremely powerful. If we treat vectors as feature representations of data points, the dot product measures how similar or aligned they are. A larger positive value indicates strong similarity, zero implies orthogonality (no relationship), and a negative value suggests opposing patterns. This directly leads to cosine similarity, defined as cos(θ) = (A · B) / (|A||B|), which is widely used in natural language processing, recommendation systems, and clustering of high-dimensional data. Furthermore, the dot product plays a crucial role in distance-based learning. Expanding vector differences gives us |A − B|² = A·A + B·B − 2A·B, which forms the basis of Euclidean distance calculations, loss functions, and optimization techniques in machine learning algorithms. Even the simplest predictive models rely on dot products; for instance, in linear regression or neural networks, predictions are computed using y = w · x + b, where the dot product between weights and input features determines the output before applying any transformation. What makes the dot product truly remarkable is that this seemingly basic operation ∑(aᵢbᵢ), powers a vast range of systems, from neural networks and embedding models like Word2Vec and BERT to financial models analyzing correlations and time-series signals. It acts as a unifying mathematical tool that connects geometry, algebra, and real-world data-driven applications. 📚 Based on handwritten-style notes inspired by lecture material #MachineLearning #DataScience #LinearAlgebra #AI #DeepLearning #Quant #Mathematics

    [3 of #100DaysOfML] How much training data is theoretically required to ensure your machine learning model will generalize reliably to unseen data from the same distribution? Let's take a look at Leslie Valiant's important 1984 paper, "A Theory of the Learnable," which later earned him the 2010 Turing Award. Before this paper, machine learning relied mostly on experiments. Valiant introduced the PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) framework, giving the field a strong theoretical base. A concept is PAC-learnable if there's an algorithm that, with high probability, finds an approximately correct hypothesis using a polynomial number of examples. For a limited set of possible models, this key result gives us a formula to estimate how much data we need: m ≥ (1/ε) * (ln|H| + ln(1/δ)) Here’s what this equation tells us: • Higher Accuracy (ε): To get your model's error rate closer to zero, you'll need more data. • Higher Confidence (δ): To be more sure your model didn't just get lucky with the training data, you need more data. • Model Complexity (|H|): If your set of possible models is bigger, you'll need more data. For continuous models such as neural networks, we use a related concept called VC Dimension, which yields a similar but mathematically distinct limit. Since classical bounds scale with model complexity, theory suggests that modern deep neural networks—with billions of parameters—should require large datasets to avoid overfitting. Yet, they still generalize really well. Figuring out why deep learning breaks these classical rules remains an exciting open question in ML theory today. #MachineLearning #DataScience #DeepLearning #100DaysOfML #AIResearch

    🚨 One small mistake can silently break a machine learning model. Not the algorithm. Not the architecture. Sometimes it's just the scale of the data. While learning about neural networks recently, I came across a simple but powerful concept: Normalization. 🧠 Consider this example Imagine training a model using two features: 👟 Daily steps → values in the thousands 😴 Sleep hours → values between 4 and 9 If we feed this directly into a model: Steps = 12000 Sleep = 7 Even if both features are important, the model may give more importance to the step count simply because the numbers are much larger. This can slow down learning and sometimes lead to unstable training. ⚙️ That’s where normalization helps A common formula used is: x' = (x − xmin) / (xmax − xmin) This rescales values into a 0–1 range, allowing features with different scales to contribute more evenly. 🖼 Example from computer vision Pixel values range from 0 to 255. Most deep learning models normalize them like this: pixel_normalized = pixel / 255 So: 128 → 0.50 255 → 1.00 ✨ A very small preprocessing step, but it can make a huge difference in how efficiently a model learns. One interesting realization for me is that sometimes the biggest improvements in machine learning don't come from changing the model, but from preparing the data properly. #GenAI #MachineLearning #DeepLearning #ArtificialIntelligence #NeuralNetworks #LearningInPublic #AIEngineering
