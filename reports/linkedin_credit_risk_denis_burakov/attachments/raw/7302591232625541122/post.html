Explainability and Scorecards 🎲 SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values have become the go-to tool for explainability in predictive modeling, and their popularity seems to be ever-growing, in part due to the lack of strong alternatives. Why? SHAP’s true hallmark lies in its ability to break down any model’s predictions into additive contributions from each input into the model. Much like Weight of Evidence (WOE) scores we know from credit scoring, SHAP values are additive and allow us to present something complex in a simpler form. But there’s a catch 🐘: SHAP values explain predictions, but not how the model actually works. Some researchers argue that because of this, SHAP scores can produce misleading information regarding feature importance and can induce bias in human decision-making, as discussed in this ACM paper: https://lnkd.in/dxHKBP5C Yet, SHAP’s additivity is a powerful tool for building scorecards that help monitor feature drift, distill complex models, and perform error analysis 🪛 Have you ever seen SHAP explanations misaligned with reality? How did you debug or validate them? Would love to hear your experiences! ⤵

    following my last post Financial returns often appear noisy or erratic but that’s only because we observe them in isolation. Their apparent randomness is, in fact, the projection of countless hidden dependencies. Markets are not a collection of independent instruments; they are systems of tacit dynamics, where each variable silently encodes the influence of all the others. In this experiment, I tried to visualize that hidden structure. By gradually strengthening or weakening the joint dynamics between assets, we can see how the behavior of returns changes once those interdependencies are (or aren’t) taken into account. The algebra beneath it? Behind this visualization lies a piece of linear algebra explained in my last post, from joint distribution X, Y we get [ X, Y ] ~ N( [ μ_X, μ_Y ], [[Σ_XX, Σ_XY], [Σ_YX, Σ_YY]] ) To actually simulate paths that respect this structure, we factorize the conditional covariance with the Cholesky decomposition: Σ_Y|X = L Lᵀ then if: z_t ~ N(0, I) ε_t = L z_t the shocks ε_t reproduce the empirical cross-asset correlations observed in the data. The simulated return vector at time t becomes: Y_t = E[Y | X_t] + ε_t In the animation, I introduced a parameter λ to blend between unstructured and fully structured shocks: ε_t(λ) = √(1−λ)·D·z₁ + √(λ)·L·z₂ where D is a diagonal matrix of individual volatilities. This interpolation allows us to explore the continuum between: λ = 0 → each asset moves independently (no structure) λ = 1 → movements follow the full Cholesky dynamic system (fully structured) As λ increases, the same random energy diffuses through the entire covariance network turning statistical noise into collective motion. In this animated simulation I used 10,000 different scenarios for each bin (2.5%), and included the following assets: SPY (S&P 500) QQQ (Nasdaq 100) XLF (Financials) XLK (Technology) XLE (Energy) XLI (Industrials) XLRE (Real Estate) MTUM (Momentum) VLUE (Value) QUAL (Quality) USMV (Min Volatility) SIZE (Small Cap)
