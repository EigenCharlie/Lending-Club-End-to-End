CHAPTER 1 Weight of Evidence: From Bletchley Park to Credit Scoring Denis Burakov In 1941, Alan Turing turned wartime guesswork into arithmetic. He took odds, logged them, and added tiny scores (decibans) until a hypothesis outweighed its rivals. Eight decades later, banks do the same thing every time you tap “Apply.” Each data point adds or subtracts a small, logarithmically-scaled score; the total crosses a threshold, and a decision falls out. This chapter follows that thread from Turing’s factor method to information theory’s view of evidence, and finally to credit scoring, showing how the same mathematics powers machines that talk, systems that learn, and the scorecards that decide our loans. 1.1 The Birth of Weight of Evidence The Turing Test and the Challenge of Evidence Alan Turing: There’s a judge, and a subject. The judge asks questions, and based on the subject’s answers, he determines: Who is he speaking with? What is he speaking with? All you have to do is ask me a question. — Graham Moore, The Imitation Game (2014) The Turing Test poses a fundamental question about judgment: How do we accumulate evidence to make a decision? How many questions must the judge ask? How much certainty is enough? These aren’t merely philosophical puzzles; they’re practical problems that Turing confronted during World War II, when the stakes were measured in lives rather than academic debates. Between 1941 and 1943, at Bletchley Park, Turing faced his own version of this challenge. Instead of judging whether a hidden subject was human or machine, he needed to judge which of millions of possible Enigma settings had encrypted the day’s German naval messages. He could not test them all as there was not enough time. He needed a method to accumulate evidence into a score, and to know when he had gathered “enough” information to make a decision. 1

Banburismus: Turing’s Bayesian Solution During the active phase of World War II, between 1941 and 1943, Turing developed a manual method for breaking Enigma codes that relied on pattern recognition and probability [13]. On a given day, Turing’s team would sit at long tables in Hut 8, aligning Banbury sheets, scoring matches, and accumulating decibans until one rotor setting outweighed the rest. His team, composed primarily of women assistants, used specially printed paper with holes punched for letters, which could be manually aligned to look for repeated patterns between pairs of mes- sages. These patterns, in turn, suggested common settings of the rotors in the Enigma machine [13]. The paper was printed in Banbury, and the entire process became known as Banburismus. The aim of “banburizing” was to suggest plausible inputs to the electromechanical Bombe computer, and to rule out other settings in order to reduce the time spent on Bombe runs [13]. The Logic of Judgment The principle of accumulating evidence through additive scoring captures something fundamen- tal about human reasoning. Long before Turing formalized the mathematics, people intuitively understood that judgment involves weighing multiple pieces of evidence until confidence crosses some threshold. Consider Bulgakov’s portrayal of Pontius Pilate interrogating Yeshua Ha-Nozri in The Mas- ter and Margarita. Pilate’s decision-making process mirrors Bayesian inference: each question and answer shifts his assessment incrementally. Yeshua’s calm demeanor provides evidence for innocence; the priests’ accusations argue for guilt; the crowd’s hostility adds noise rather than signal. Pilate accumulates this evidence mentally, his confidence shifting with each exchange, until political necessity forces a decision. This intuitive process—accumulating evidence until a threshold is crossed—appears across human judgment contexts. British courts recognize it implicitly: individual pieces of evidence are admissible, but juries cannot systematically multiply likelihood ratios. The law insists that combining evidence must remain human judgment, not mathematical formula. Turing’s breakthrough was making this implicit process explicit. At Bletchley Park, he con- verted judgment into arithmetic: evidence could be accumulated systematically, scores could be tallied, and decisions could be reached when thresholds were crossed. What had been intuitive became mechanical, what had been subjective became reproducible. 1.2 The Mathematical Foundation Having seen how Banburismus worked in practice, we can now examine the mathematical prin- ciples that made it so effective.

THE MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATION 5 The Factor Principle: Bayes’ Theorem in Logarithmic Form The mathematical elegance of Banburismus lies in its systematic treatment of hypothesis testing through what Turing called the Factor Principle [1]. This principle provides a framework for accumulating evidence that remains fundamental to modern statistical inference and machine learning. We start with a hypothesis to be tested, for example, that a certain statement is true. We also start with some understanding of the credibility of the hypothesis, which may be objective or subjective. We can express that credibility in terms of odds. Odds are straightforward: if something happens on average four times out of five trials (corresponding to an 80% probability), the odds are 4:1 in favor of its happening (or 1:4 against its not happening) [1]. What we start with are the prior odds, prior, that is, to an event that gives us some additional evidence. We now need to know whether that event is more likely to happen when the hypothesis is true or when it is false, and how much more likely. More precisely, we need the ratio of these two probabilities. This ratio is called the “Bayes factor” or, in Turing’s terminology, “the factor” [1]. Bayes’ theorem tells us that the credibility after we have the additional evidence (the posterior odds) is obtained by multiplying the prior odds by the Bayes factor: Posterior odds = Prior odds × Bayes factor (1.1) where Bayes factor = P (evidence|hypothesis true) P (evidence|hypothesis false) (1.2) The credibility is enhanced or diminished according to whether the factor is above or be- low 1. Moreover, if there are successive pieces of evidence that are independent of each other, their factors can be multiplied to give a composite factor [1]. This multiplicative property is the foundation of what we now call the naive Bayes classifier [10]. This relationship can also be written in the formal “odds form” of Bayes’ theorem: O(H | E) = O(H)× P (E | H) P (E | H̄) , where O(H | E) and O(H) denote the posterior and prior odds respectively, and P (E|H) P (E|H̄) is the Bayes factor or, in Turing’s terms, the factor in favor of H provided by E [3]. Turing’s innovation was to work with logarithms of these factors. Taking logarithms of both sides: log(Posterior odds) = log(Prior odds) + log(Bayes factor) (1.3) This transformation converts multiplication into addition, making the calculations tractable for manual computation. Shannon’s information content measures how surprising an event is; Turing’s weight of evidence measures how strongly an observation shifts belief. Both quantify change in knowl- edge using logarithms of probability. Where Turing’s concern was inference (deciding between hypotheses), Shannon’s was communication, i.e. distinguishing messages. Their mathematics, however, is identical. Later writers such as I.J. Good and David MacKay would make this unity explicit: inference is information processing. The connection between Banburismus and modern information theory is profound [5]. When we compare hypotheses with each other in the light of data, it is often convenient to compare the log of the probability of the data under the alternative hypotheses: log evidence for Hi = logP (D|Hi) (1.4) Or, in the case where just two hypotheses are being compared, we evaluate the ‘log odds ratio’: log P (D|H1) P (D|H2) (1.5) This quantity is the ‘weight of evidence in favor of H1’ [5]. The log evidence for a hypothesis, logP (D|Hi), is the negative of the information content of the data D under that hypothesis: if the data have large information content given a hypothesis, then they are surprising to that hypothesis; if some other hypothesis is not as surprised by the data, then that hypothesis becomes more probable [5]. As David MacKay elegantly notes, ‘information content’, ‘surprise value’, and log-likelihood (or log evidence) are the same thing [5]. This unification connects Shannon’s information theory [7] with Bayesian inference and provides the theoretical foundation for modern machine learning algorithms. Formally, information theory quantifies the notion of “surprise.” The information content (or self-information) of an outcome x, with probability P (x), is de- fined as h(x) = − logP (x). (1.6) Unlikely events (small P (x)) have high information content, meaning they are more surpris- ing. To understand this intuitively, consider a coin that lands heads 90% of the time [11]. We can express the surprise of an outcome as 1/P (x), but Shannon showed it is better to define it as the logarithm: log(1/P (x)) = − logP (x). This logarithmic measure, known as Shannon information or surprisal, has the crucial property that surprises from independent events can be added rather than multiplied. The entropy of a random variable X , measuring the expected surprise across all outcomes, is defined as H(X) = − ∑ x P (x) logP (x). (1.7)

The Historical Context and Legacy Neither Alexander’s nor Mahon’s contemporaneous histories mentioned the word ‘Bayes’, and Turing himself generally referred to “the factor” [1]. I.J. Good also recalls that: “When I said to him that the concept was essentially an application of Bayes’s theorem he said ‘I suppose so’ ” [4]. In Turing’s later declassified paper [13], we can see Factor Principle (Bayes’ Theorem). The historical significance of the ban as a unit of measurement cannot be overstated [5]. When Alan Turing and the other codebreakers at Bletchley Park were breaking each new day’s Enigma code, their task was a massive inference problem: to infer, given the day’s cyphertext, which three wheels were in the Enigma machines that day; what their starting positions were; what further letter substitutions were in use on the steckerboard; and, not least, what the original German messages were [5]. These inferences were conducted using Bayesian methods, and the chosen units were decibans or half-decibans, with the deciban judged to be the smallest weight of evidence discernible to human intuition [5]. The evidence in favor of particular hypotheses was tallied using sheets of paper that were specially printed in Banbury. The inference task was known as Banburismus, and the units in which Banburismus was played were called bans, after that town [5]. Using increasingly sophisticated language models, the evidence contributed by runs of matches was more accurately computed [5]. Such a scoring system was worked out by Turing and refined by Good. Positive results were passed on to automated and human-powered codebreakers. Ac- cording to Good, the longest false-positive that arose in this work was a string of 8 consecutive matches between two messages that were actually in unrelated states [5], a testament to the ro- bustness of the methodology. From Decibans to WOE: The Mathematical Bridge To understand how Turing’s wartime innovations connect to modern credit scoring, we need to examine the Weight of Evidence (WOE) transformation, a staple of credit risk modeling that has its roots directly in the Factor Principle. Weight of Evidence quantifies the strength of evidence supporting or refuting a hypothesis, derived from independent pieces of data. The mathematical foundation, drawn directly from Turing’s declassified 1941 paper [13], is: A posteriori odds of the theory = A priori odds of the theory × Probability of the data being fulfilled if the theory is true Probability of the data being fulfilled if the theory is false (1.10) In this formula, the theory is that the man died of heart failure, and the data is that he died in his bed. The general formula above is described as the factor principle. The ratio Probability of the data if the theory is true Probability of the data if the theory is false (1.11) is what Turing called the factor for the theory on account of the data. This factor later became known as the Bayes factor [1].

The Independence Assumption and Its Limitations The mathematical foundation for combining independent evidence has an interesting history. Charles Sanders Peirce introduced the concept in 1878, showing how to add weights of evidence from independent sources. However, Peirce made a subtle but crucial error: he summed evidence without properly accounting for prior odds. This oversight wasn’t corrected until Turing and Good’s wartime work established the rigorous mathematical framework that underlies modern additive scoring systems (see bibliographical notes for the full story). The naive Bayes assumption (that features contribute independent evidence) is both the great strength and the primary limitation of WOE-based scoring. This assumption allows for simple additive scoring and makes models highly interpretable, but this assumption breaks down when features are correlated or interact in complex ways. The principles that helped defeat Enigma (accumulating evidence through logarithmic scoring, making decisions under uncertainty, and balancing accuracy with computational efficiency) re- main central to how banks make lending decisions today. The methods that helped win World War II have evolved into the algorithms that help us make better decisions about risk, opportunity, and the future. Turing Among the many figures who shaped twentieth-century science, few had a deeper influence on the foundations of modern data analysis than Ronald A. Fisher and Alan M. Turing. Both were British prodigies who transformed how knowledge could be extracted from uncertainty. Fisher formalized the logic of inference, turning statistics into an exact science, while Turing redefined reasoning itself through computation and probability. Though they never collaborated directly, their intellectual legacies form complementary parts of a single story. Ronald A. Fisher and Alan Turing crossed paths in the mid-1930s when Turing submitted his fellowship dissertation at King’s College, Cambridge. Fisher, already a leading figure in statistics, was one of the examiners. He found the subject unappealing and dismissed it as overly explored, but nevertheless acknowledged the originality of Turing’s methods and endorsed him as a can- didate of real distinction. The irony is that while Fisher had once judged Turing’s mathematics with skepticism, Turing’s secret wartime breakthroughs relied on ideas Fisher would have firmly rejected, though Fisher never knew it at the time due to secrecy of Turing’s work at Bletchley Park. Good In The Probability of Induction (1878), Charles Sanders Peirce provided one of the earliest formu- lations of what later became known as the weight of evidence. He argued that belief should grow in proportion to the strength of independent arguments. Charles Sanders Peirce wanted a way to measure how strongly independent arguments sup- port a hypothesis — what he called the intensity of belief. He reasoned like this: “If two independent arguments both support the same conclusion, then the total belief should be the sum of the beliefs from each.” That passage is one of the earliest known formal definitions of weight of evidence as an additive quantity in log-odds space, nearly seventy years before Turing made the same principle operational for Bayesian intelligence and statistical inference. He then added: “Since independent arguments multiply their chances, the feeling of belief should be proportional to the logarithm of the chance.” The rule for the combination of independent concurrent arguments takes a very simple form when expressed in terms of the intensity of belief, measured in the proposed way:

This is a proceeding which men often resort to, under the name of balancing reasons.” Peirce observed that when independent pieces of evidence each favor a hypothesis to some degree, our total belief should combine additively in the scale of feeling or belief — even though, mathematically, probabilities themselves combine multiplicatively. He proposed that the appro- priate “thermometer” for belief intensity must therefore be the logarithm of the odds (chance). Almost a century later, I.J. Good re-examined Peirce’s argument in his short 1981 note “An Error by Peirce Concerning ‘Weight of Evidence”’ [2]. Good agreed that Peirce’s intuition was brilliant but showed that the algebra behind it was wrong. Peirce had written the independence of evidence as: P (H | E,F ) = P (H | E)P (H | F ) (1.29) which implies O(H | E,F ) = O(H | E)O(H | F ) (1.30) where O(H) denotes the odds of the hypothesis H . That equation holds only if the prior odds O(H) = 1, i.e. a 50% prior probability, an implicit assumption Peirce never stated. Translating to today’s methodology, Peirce effectively summed two weights of evidence with- out including any prior base rate, the intercept term, representing the prior odds before any evidence is observed. Good pointed out that the correct independence condition is not on the posteriors but on the likelihoods: P (E,F | H) = P (E | H)P (F | H) (1.31) P (E,F | H̄) = P (E | H̄)P (F | H̄) (1.32) From Bayes’ theorem, O(H | E,F ) = O(H) × P (E | H) P (E | H̄) × P (F | H) P (F | H̄) . (1.33) Taking logarithms gives the additive law that Peirce intuited: logO(H | E,F ) = logO(H) + log P (E | H) P (E | H̄) + log P (F | H) P (F | H̄) . (1.34) Each term is a weight of evidence — the log-likelihood ratio contributed by one piece of data. This mathematical framework, refined over more than a century from Peirce’s initial insight through Good’s correction, provides the theoretical foundation for modern credit scoring sys- tems. The same principles that Peirce intuited for combining evidence now power the algorithms that process millions of loan applications, demonstrating the enduring value of fundamental mathematical insights.
