📘 𝘓𝘰𝘨𝘪𝘴𝘵𝘪𝘤 𝘙𝘦𝘨𝘳𝘦𝘴𝘴𝘪𝘰𝘯: 𝘍𝘳𝘰𝘮 𝘔𝘢𝘹𝘪𝘮𝘶𝘮 𝘓𝘪𝘬𝘦𝘭𝘪𝘩𝘰𝘰𝘥 𝘵𝘰 𝘔𝘢𝘤𝘩𝘪𝘯𝘦 𝘓𝘦𝘢𝘳𝘯𝘪𝘯𝘨 What Alan Turing did for reasoning under uncertainty, Ronald Fisher did for learning from data. His 1922 breakthrough, the method of maximum likelihood, transformed a bewildering array of disconnected statistical techniques into a unified framework. It became the first real bridge between classical statistics and what we now call machine learning. You'll meet the pioneers who built the foundation of every modern predictive model: Chester Bliss's probit analysis in the 1930s, Joseph Berkson's elegant logistic simplification, and Fisher's likelihood principle that now powers the softmax function at the heart of language models like Claude and ChatGPT. This is the story of how classical statistical inference built a bridge to machine learning, with the historical details that will reveal they were never as separate as we thought. 👉Inside: hand-solved examples that build intuition, the mathematical equivalence between WOE and logistic coefficients, practical gradient descent for PD calibration, and extensions to multiclass problems with modern uncertainty quantification. Grateful to Andrija Djurovic for his thoughtful feedback on earlier drafts, and to Adrian Olszewski whose materials on logistic regression helped me shape my thinking throughout. #Statistics #CreditRisk #DataScience #MachineLearning #AI

Here’s the mental model that changed how I approach AI forever: When you ask an LLM a question, it doesn’t “think.” It doesn’t “retrieve.” It doesn’t “look up.” It simply runs one loop: “Given all patterns I’ve seen before… what is the most probable next token?” That’s the entire intelligence.     Why Today's "Reasoning Models" Don't Actually Reason Despite their impressive capabilities, today's LLMs don't truly reason. They predict tokens based on statistical patterns. Ask a model "What is 3+5?" and it excels. Why? It's seen this pattern countless times in training data. But present something less common, like solving "3^x = x^9", and the limitations emerge. (try it out yourself!) True reasoning requires manipulating abstract symbols according to mathematical principles, not just pattern matching. So what's Missing? Genuine reasoning needs a symbolic workspace: a place where concepts can be explicitly manipulated, tested, and transformed. Current models lack this fundamental architecture. I believe the breakthrough will come from two directions: 1. Neuro-symbolic hybrids that combine neural prediction with explicit rule systems 2. Visual grounding through computer vision, allowing AI to build common sense by experiencing the physical world directly The contextual richness of visual experience could be what moves us beyond statistical mimicry toward authentic reasoning. What's your take? Are we asking the wrong questions about AI reasoning, or are hybrid approaches the answer? #AI #LLM #AIReasoning #NeuralNetworks #ComputerVision

    Inside the Machine: How Retrosynthesis AI Thinks Like a Chemist Imagine describing a molecule to an AI and watching it think backward — step by step — to tell you how to make it. That’s retrosynthesis, reimagined through machine learning. Traditional retrosynthesis mimics human reasoning: you identify the most strategic bond to break, recall similar reactions, and trace backward to known starting materials. AI does the same — but at a scale no human can match. 🧠 How it works: ASKCOS encodes thousands of reaction templates and applies them recursively to build possible routes. IBM RXN for Chemistry uses transformer models (similar to GPT) to predict reaction outcomes from SMILES strings. Reaxys supplies verified experimental data to score and rank the realism of each route. Every path becomes a reaction tree, where each branch represents a synthesis possibility — merging logic, data, and chemistry intuition into one system. And because these systems learn from real literature, they inherit the creativity of generations of chemists.
