CHAPTER 2 Logistic Regression: From Maximum Likelihood to Machine Learning Denis Burakov1 To understand the origins of logistic regression, we must journey back to the early 1920s, when the field of statistics was, in Frank Yates’ memorable phrase, “the age of correlation and coefficients of all kinds” [20]. By 1920, statisticians had accumulated a bewildering array of techniques—least squares, method of moments, inverse probability, the χ2 test, Pearson’s system of curves—but lacked what Yudi Pawitan calls “a firm logical foundation.” As Dermot Turing [24] observed, “The only orthodox way to measure probabilities was to take samples and observe their distribution, in the manner leading to the good old Gaussian Error Function.” Into this confusion stepped Ronald A. The path from Fisher’s theoretical breakthrough to today’s ubiquitous logistic regression winds through three distinct phases: the establishment of maximum likelihood as the gold standard for parameter estimation, the development of probit analysis for dose-response studies, and finally Joseph Berkson’s elegant simplification that gave us the logistic function. This method had fallen into disrepute due to philosophical objections about prior probabilities. Into this theoretical void came Fisher’s insight, which he had been developing since his early 1912 paper On an Absolute Criterion for Fitting Frequency Curves [9]: instead of asking “What is the probability that the parameter equals θ given the data?” (the inverse probability approach), we should ask “What is the probability of observing this data given that the parameter equals θ?” (the likelihood approach). This seemingly subtle shift revolutionized statistical inference. Fisher’s influence would prove so profound that, as I.J. Good [13] later noted, “In 1950 the Bayesian approach was unpopular, largely because of Fisher’s influence.” Even Harold Jeffreys, one of the founders of modern Bayesian statistics, acknowledged Fisher’s contribution in his influential Theory of Probability [15], describing likelihood as “a convenient term introduced by Professor R. A. Fisher.”

By 1940, probit analysis had become the standard method for analyzing binary response data in biology, agriculture, and medicine. LOGISTIC REGRESSION: FROM MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD TO MACHINE LEARNING The Gradient Descent Approach The logistic function’s non-linearity in its parameters means that, except for special cases such as binary predictors with grouped data, we cannot solve for maximum likelihood estimates in closed form. Instead, logistic regression relies on iterative optimization methods that follow the gradient of the log-likelihood to find the parameter values that maximize it. To see how this works in practice, consider a simulated dataset with 200 loan applications where we calibrate the internal bank probability of default (PD) as a function of a credit bureau score expressed in log-odds form—that is, the log of the external PD. This setup reflects a common scenario in which internal risk drivers are unavailable or limited, so an external PD estimate is recalibrated to align with a lender’s internal default experience. This model formulation operates naturally in the log-odds space—the canonical scale of logistic regression. The functional form of the calibration model is as follows: • Intercept: β0 = −2.0 (log-odds of default at the reference score) • Slope: β1 = 1.5 (increase in log-odds of default for each one-unit increase in score) Because higher probabilities of default correspond to higher risk, β1 is positive. At the reference score (X = 0), the default probability is e−2.0 1+e−2.0 ≈ 0.12 (12%). Each one-unit increase in score multiplies the odds of default by e1.5 ≈ 5. The data is generated such that the probability of default follows: P (y = 1|X) = 1 1 + e−(β0+β1X) (2.41) Our goal is to recover these true parameters from the observed data using gradient descent. To incorporate an intercept term, we augment the design matrix X with a column of ones, so each observation has the form xi = [1, xi]. We initialize the parameters at β0 = 0 and β1 = 0, giving β = [0, 0]. This initialization corresponds to a model that predicts P (y = 1) = 0.5 for all observations, regardless of their feature values—treating both outcomes as equally likely. From this starting point, gradient descent will iteratively adjust the parameters to incorporate the observed patterns in the data, moving toward the maximum likelihood estimates. The gradient descent algorithm follows a straightforward iterative logic: compute predictions using the current parameters, measure the discrepancy between predictions and observations, and update the parameters to reduce this discrepancy. Each iteration moves the parameters in the direction that most improves the fit to the data, as measured by the log-likelihood. The process continues until the parameters stabilize at values that maximize the likelihood of observing the data. The mechanics of this procedure unfold in three steps.

Yet, most applied practitioners view Weight of Evidence (WOE) as merely a feature encoding step, with logistic regression doing the “real” work. But this is a misconception: WOE transformations capture predictive power through likelihood ratios, while logistic regression adjusts for dependencies among features. The mathematical reality: Under the naive Bayes assumption (features are independent given the class), WOE values can be seen as stand-alone univariate logistic regression models: log P (Y = 1|X) P (Y = 0|X) = log P (Y = 1) P (Y = 0) + p∑ j=1 WOEj(xj) (2.44) Each WOEj(xj) = log p(xj |Y=1) p(xj |Y=0) is a likelihood ratio that quantifies how much evidence feature j provides for class 1 versus class 0a. Yet, in practice, features are often correlated. Spiegelhalter and Knill-Jones [22] found that in medical diagnostics, symptoms and test results are often correlated, leading to overconfident predictions when using raw WOE sums. Their solution was to use WOE values as inputs to logistic regression: log P (Y = 1|X) P (Y = 0|X) = β0 + p∑ j=1 βj × WOEj(xj) (2.45) Here, the coefficients βj serve as adjustment factors that account for feature dependencies. If features were truly independent, we would expect βj ≈ 1 for all j. Deviations from 1 indicate the degree to which the naive independence assumption is violated. In essence, WOE captures each feature’s univariate predictive signal through likelihood ratios, while logistic regression recalibrates these signals to prevent double-counting correlated evidence. An ensemble perspective: Each WOE value corresponds to a univariate logistic regression that captures the relationship between one predictor and the outcome, expressed in log- odds space. The final logistic regression then acts as a meta-learner that combines these univariate predictions: Feature Univariate model Meta-learner x1, . . . , xp WOE1, . . . ,WOEp β0 + ∑ j βj WOEj Each univariate model contributes its "vote" (the WOE value), and the meta-learner (logistic regression) learns how much weight to give each vote, accounting for redundancy when features are correlated. This is why the method remains competitive with modern machine learning: it effectively combines strong univariate feature engineering with flexible multivariate calibration. aIn credit risk texts, the fraction is often inverted so that higher scores correspond to better credit quality, which reverses the sign of the resulting coefficients.

Logistic regression in this setting models the log-odds of one outcome relative to the other. However, many real-world problems involve more than two possible outcomes: customers may be classified into a number of segments, transactions may belong to several fraud types—such as first-party fraud or account takeover—and a loan may end up in one of several delinquency states—a topic central to collections scoring. A note on Ordinal Outcomes When outcomes have a natural ordering (e.g., credit ratings from AAA to D, or delinquency buckets like current, 30+ days past due, 60+ days past due, 90+ days past due), ordinal logistic regression models can be more appropriate than standard multinomial models. These models, particularly common in low-default portfolio modeling, respect the ordering by modeling cumulative probabilities rather than treating categories as unordered. While we focus on multinomial logistic regression in this chapter, the fundamental principles—maximum likelihood estimation, log-odds transformations, and the connection to WOE—extend naturally to ordinal models. Beyond credit risk and finance, the same principle underlies state-of-the-art machine learning systems. For instance, large language models predict the probability distribution over thousands of possible next tokens, which is an inherently multinomial classification problem. The logic of logistic regression extends naturally to these settings. This generalization from the binomial to the multinomial was already implicit in early probability theory. Ronald Fisher, with his characteristic insight, recognized that the binomial distribution—formalized by Jacob Bernoulli and studied for over two centuries—was merely a special case of a broader family: the multinomial. As Fisher [11] wrote with characteristic precision: “The binomial distribution... was found by Bernoulli... that if the probability of an event occurring were p and the probability of it not occurring were q = 1− p, then if a random sample of n trials were taken, the frequencies with which the event occurred 0, 1, 2, ..., n times were given by the expansion of the binomial (q + p)n. This rule is a particular case of a more general theorem dealing with cases in which not only a simple alternative is considered, but in which the event may happen in s ways with probabilities p1, p2, . . . , ps." This insight—recognizing the binomial as a special case of the multinomial distribution— directly motivates the extension from binary logistic regression to multinomial logistic regression.

LOGISTIC REGRESSION: FROM MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD TO MACHINE LEARNING Interpreting Coverage for Probability Intervals Before we proceed to the discussion of evaluating predictive intervals for probabilities, it is important to clarify what “coverage” means. A probability prediction interval such as [p̂L, p̂U ] is not meant to contain the binary outcome yi ∈ {0, 1}, but rather to provide valid uncertainty quantification for the true conditional probability p(xi) = Pr(Y = 1 | X = xi). Since individual-level true probabilities are unobservable, coverage is assessed through empirical event rates: • Binary coverage — the proportion of cases where yi ∈ [p̂L, p̂U ] — is usually near 0% or 100%, depending on whether intervals are narrow or span most of [0, 1]. It is not an appropriate metric for probability intervals. • Conditional coverage — the key validity criterion — requires that within groups of similar predictions, the observed event rate falls within the predicted interval approximately (1 − α) of the time. Formally: Pr(p(X) ∈ [p̂L(X), p̂U(X)] | X ∈ S) ≥ 1− α for subsets S of the feature space. • Marginal coverage — a weaker but still valid guarantee — requires that averaged over the test distribution, intervals contain true probabilities with frequency (1 − α): Pr(p(X) ∈ [p̂L(X), p̂U(X)]) ≥ 1− α. Note on standard conformal prediction: Classical conformal prediction for binary classifica- tion produces prediction sets such as {“default”}, {“not_default”}, or {“default”, “not_default”} with coverage guarantees for the true class label. In credit risk, however, we need probability intervals that quantify uncertainty in p̂i itself, not just class membership. This requires conformalized approaches that adapt the conformal framework to continuous probability estimates. Intuitively, valid coverage for probability intervals answers: “When I predict uncertainty bounds [p̂L, p̂U ] for groups of similar cases, how often does the observed event rate in those groups fall inside my stated interval?” This distinction explains why properly calibrated methods can yield wide intervals with zero binary coverage yet still provide valid uncertainty quantification—they are calibrated to cover true underlying probabilities, not discrete outcomes. While logistic regression provides point estimates of probabilities, real-world applica- tions—particularly in credit risk and regulatory compliance—require understanding the confi- dence we can place in these estimates. This section introduces two complementary approaches to quantifying this uncertainty: • Wald-type confidence intervals: The classical approach, derived from the estimated covariance matrix of the coefficients. This method leverages the asymptotic normality of maximum likelihood estimators [14]. • Conformalized prediction: A modern, distribution-free approach that uses empirical residuals to construct probability intervals with guaranteed coverage properties. Wald-Type Confidence Intervals for Logistic Regression The most widely used method for inference in logistic regression relies on the asymptotic normality of the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs). Under regularity conditions, the estimated coefficients β̂ are approximately normal with covariance matrix equal to the inverse of the observed information matrix. The key intuition: We treat the model parameters as random variables centered around their estimated values, with uncertainty captured by the curvature of the likelihood surface. A sharply peaked likelihood (high curvature) indicates we have strong information about the parameters, leading to narrow confidence intervals. A flat likelihood (low curvature) indicates greater uncertainty and wider intervals. In logistic regression, the model updates during estimation are given by: β(t+1) = β(t) + [ X⊤W(t)X ]−1 X⊤(y − p(t)), (2.87) where W(t) is a diagonal matrix with elements wi = p̂ (t) i (1 − p̂ (t) i ). At convergence, the Fisher Information Matrix is: I(β) = X⊤WX, (2.88) and the estimated covariance matrix of the coefficient estimates is its inverse: Cov(β̂) = I(β)−1 = [ X⊤WX ]−1 . (2.89) This inverse relationship reflects a fundamental principle: the more information we have about the parameters (higher Fisher Information), the lower the variance of our estimates. In practice, this covariance matrix is automatically computed by statistical software during model fitting. The diagonal elements give the variances of individual coefficients, while off- diagonal elements capture correlations between coefficient estimates. From coefficients to predictions: To construct confidence intervals for a new prediction, we need to propagate the uncertainty in β̂ through to the predicted probability. This involves two steps: first, quantifying uncertainty in the logit (linear predictor), then transforming to the probability scale. They form the basis of what are commonly known as Wald-type confidence intervals, implemented by default in most statistical software packages (e.g., R’s predict.glm() with se.fit = TRUE, Python’s statsmodels.GLM.get_prediction(), and SAS’s PROC LOGISTIC). Practical considerations: Wald intervals work well when sample sizes are large and the model is well-specified. However, with very large samples, intervals can become narrow and may be overly optimistic, failing to account for model misspecification or systematic biases. Additionally, they can be unreliable in small samples or when predicted probabilities are near 0 or 1 (where the logistic transformation is highly nonlinear). This motivates the alternative approach we explore next: conformalized prediction. Conformalized Prediction via Pearson Residuals While Wald intervals rely on asymptotic normality and model correctness, conformalized prediction provides an alternative, distribution-free approach. Rather than relying on theoretical assumptions, it uses empirical nonconformity scores11 from a calibration dataset to construct probability intervals that achieve valid coverage in finite samples. The fundamental shift: Instead of asking "what does theory tell us about parameter uncer- tainty?", conformalized prediction asks "how wrong was our model on past data, and how can we use that to quantify future uncertainty?" This shifts the focus from parametric inference to data-driven calibration of uncertainty. The general form of studentized residuals is: Ri = |Yi − f̂n1(Xi)| σ̂n1(Xi) , i ∈ D2, where f̂n1(Xi) is the point predictor and σ̂n1(Xi) is the spread predictor (estimated standard deviation) fitted on training set D1 and evaluated on calibration set D2. The key insight is to normalize the raw prediction error |yi − p̂i| by a "spread predictor" that estimates the variability at each point. For binary outcomes, the natural spread predictor is √ p̂i(1− p̂i), which is the standard deviation of a Bernoulli random variable. This normalization makes residuals comparable across different predicted probabilities: a prediction error of 0.1 at p̂ = 0.5 (high variance region) is treated differently than the same error at p̂ = 0.95 (low variance region). The resulting conformalized probability interval (confidence band) for a new observation is: [p̂L, p̂U ] = [ max ( 0, p̂− q1−α √ p̂(1− p̂) ) , min ( 1, p̂+ q1−α √ p̂(1− p̂) )] . Starting from the Pearson residual ri = (yi − p̂i)/ √ p̂i(1− p̂i), the nonconformity score is computed as: si = ∣∣∣∣∣ ri√ p̂i(1− p̂i) ∣∣∣∣∣ = ∣∣∣∣ yi − p̂i p̂i(1− p̂i) ∣∣∣∣ . Since p̂i(1 − p̂i) is the derivative of the logistic function, si represents the local linear approximation of the logit shift needed to align predictions with observed outcomes. After computing the calibration quantile q1−α = Quantile1−α({si}i∈Ical), we construct symmetric intervals on the logit scale: ηi = logit(p̂i) = log ( p̂i 1− p̂i ) , [ηL, ηU ] = [ ηi − q1−α, ηi + q1−α ]. We then transform back to the probability scale: [p̂L, p̂U ] = [ σ(ηL), σ(ηU) ] , where σ(z) = 1 1 + e−z . This logit-based conformalized method produces intervals that respect the natural geometry of the logistic model and automatically remain within [0, 1] without clipping. The intervals are wider near p̂i = 0.5 and narrower near the boundaries, reflecting the nonlinear structure of the logit scale. Like the Pearson-based approach, this method provides valid conformalized coverage guaran- tees under the exchangeability assumption (see Figure 2.7). In essence, conformalized prediction turns residual variability into predictive uncertainty, offering valid coverage guarantees without relying on asymptotic or parametric assumptions. Terminology note: In classification, we construct probability intervals or confidence bands for p̂i, not prediction intervals for the binary outcome yi ∈ {0, 1}. Prediction intervals are typically reserved for continuous regression outcomes, where we predict a range for the actual value. Here, we quantify uncertainty about the probability estimate itself. Why Pearson residuals? The Pearson residual standardizes the prediction error by the standard deviation of a Bernoulli random variable, making residuals comparable across different predicted probabilities. A prediction of 0.5 with an error of 0.1 is treated differently than a prediction of 0.95 with the same error, because the latter represents a more surprising deviation. Practical advantages: Conformalized prediction provides finite-sample validity guarantees and works with any model (not just logistic regression). The main cost is requiring a separate calibration dataset, which reduces the effective training sample size. In applications where data is abundant and coverage guarantees are critical—such as regulatory compliance—this trade-off is often worthwhile.

On the probability scale, the corresponding Pearson-based intervals achieve approximately 95% empirical coverage but are wider and more asymmetric. 2.7 Later Developments: From Statistics to Machine Learning By the 1970s, logistic regression had established itself as the standard approach for binary classification in statistics. What followed was a remarkable transformation: the migration of these ideas from statistical inference to computational learning, and ultimately to the neural networks that dominate modern machine learning. The Computational Revolution (1970s-1980s) The 1970s marked a turning point where logistic regression transitioned from a statistical technique requiring manual calculation to a computational tool. Two developments were particularly significant: Generalized Linear Models (1972): Nelder and Wedderburn [19] introduced the GLM framework, unifying logistic regression with other models through the concept of link functions. This abstraction revealed that logistic regression was not an isolated technique but part of a broader mathematical structure. The GLM framework provided efficient iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) algorithms that made fitting logistic models computationally tractable. Discrete Choice Theory: Daniel McFadden’s work on multinomial logistic regression and discrete choice analysis [18] extended logistic regression beyond binary outcomes. His theoretical contributions, which earned him the Nobel Prize in Economics (2000), showed how the logistic function naturally emerges from assumptions about random utility maximization. This connected statistical modeling to economic theory and opened new applications in transportation, marketing, and policy analysis.

54 References The Machine Learning Era (1980s-2000s) As computing power increased, logistic regression found new life in machine learning, where the focus shifted from inference to prediction: Regularization Methods: The introduction of ridge regression and later the LASSO (Least Ab- solute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) in the 1990s addressed overfitting in high-dimensional settings. Regularized logistic regression became essential for handling datasets where the number of features approached or exceeded the number of observations—a common scenario in genomics, text classification, and early web applications. Ensemble Methods: The 2000s saw logistic regression incorporated into ensemble frameworks. Boosting algorithms like AdaBoost and later gradient boosting machines combined multiple weak learners (often decision stumps) with logistic loss functions. This marriage of tree-based methods and logistic regression produced some of the most successful predictive models in practice. Large-Scale Applications: The rise of internet companies in the late 1990s and 2000s created unprecedented demand for scalable classification algorithms. Logistic regression, with its convex loss function and efficient optimization, became the workhorse of spam filtering, ad click prediction, and recommendation systems. Companies like Google and Facebook deployed logistic regression models on billions of data points, driving innovations in distributed computing. Contemporary Perspectives Today, when you apply for a credit card and receive an instant decision, when your email filters spam with uncanny accuracy, or when a medical algorithm helps diagnose disease, you’re witness- ing the culmination of this remarkable journey. From Fisher’s maximum likelihood framework to Berkson’s logistic simplification, from Cox’s mathematical refinements to McFadden’s economic applications, these contributions have evolved into the algorithms that power modern decisioning. The logistic function—an “underdog” once dismissed as inferior to its probit rival—now powers the decision engines of the modern economy. In the end, Berkson’s vision proved prophetic: sometimes the “inferior” alternative becomes the foundation upon which entire multi-billion industries are built.
