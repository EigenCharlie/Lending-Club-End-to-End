You'll often see claims that Gini score isn't suitable for imbalanced problems. Credit risk problems are imbalanced by nature (if your default rate > 50%, the business probably isn't there anymore), and Gini is exactly the right metric in this setting. Take as an example a portfolio with a ~10% default rate and relatively strong credit score. Here's what a CAP diagram will show: • 𝘕𝘶𝘮𝘦𝘳𝘢𝘵𝘰𝘳 (0.469): Area between model and random line • 𝘋𝘦𝘯𝘰𝘮𝘪𝘯𝘢𝘵𝘰𝘳 (1−DR = 0.903): Maximum achievable area given DR • 𝘎𝘪𝘯𝘪 (Accuracy Ratio): 0.469 / 0.903 = 51.9% The DR adjustment in this formula is what enables fair comparison across portfolios with different default rates. With the same raw ranking power (numerator = 0.4), a model achieving 42% Gini at 5% DR (0.4/0.95) is actually weaker than one achieving 50% Gini at 20% DR (0.4/0.80). Accounting for class imbalance through the DR constraint is what makes Gini the standard for measuring model discrimination in credit risk. #CreditRisk #DataScience #CreditScoring #ModelRisk #ModelValidation

I completely agree. This misconception appears surprisingly often. ROC-AUC evaluates the model’s ranking ability — the probability that a randomly chosen positive receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative. Because it is based on relative ranking rather than class counts, it is invariant to class imbalance. What imbalance actually affects are operational metrics such as precision, recall, or business thresholds, not the model’s intrinsic discriminatory power captured by ROC-AUC (or equivalently the Gini coefficient).

Abstract: The classical credibility premium provides a simple and efficient method for predicting future damages and losses.
