    🔍 Mastering BSTs with Real-World Range Queries! 🌳 Today I tackled an interesting problem involving Binary Search Trees (BSTs): 👉 Given a BST and a range [l, r], calculate the sum of all nodes whose values fall within this range. At first glance, this might seem like a simple tree traversal. But here's where understanding the BST property can make your solution significantly more efficient. 💡 Optimization Insight: Since BSTs maintain sorted order — where all left children are smaller and right children are larger — we can prune entire subtrees during traversal: If the current node’s value is less than l, we can ignore its left subtree. If it’s greater than r, we can skip its right subtree. This drastically reduces the time complexity, especially in large or balanced trees. 🔄 Instead of blindly visiting every node, we leverage structure to guide our path — a great reminder that understanding the underlying data structure is often more powerful than brute force. 🎯 Takeaway: Smart traversal beats full traversal.     🔓 While working on some extensions for my Market-Regime project, I came across a fascinating algorithm: 𝐌𝐞𝐭𝐫𝐨𝐩𝐨𝐥𝐢𝐬–𝐇𝐚𝐬𝐭𝐢𝐧𝐠𝐬: a practical way to explore complex probability distributions without ever computing the normalization constant. No normalization constant? That's exciting! Even when the true probability is only known up to a constant, we can still draw realistic samples. Each step proposes a nearby point and accepts it with a probability based on how much "better" the target function is there - the unknown constant simply cancels out. After a short burn-in, the chain mixes and naturally spends more time in the denser regions of the distribution, giving us a solid empirical picture even for multi-modal shapes. A few reasons it's so practical: 𝟏. No gradients, no normalization. Just provide an unnormalized target and a simple symmetric proposal. 𝟐. You can tune the proposal scale to keep a healthy acceptance rate. 𝟑. It is great for estimating expectations, marginal behavior, and for Bayesian inference when models get big. ** Click on the GIF to get the full visualization.
