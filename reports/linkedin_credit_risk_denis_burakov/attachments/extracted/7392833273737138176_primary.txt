    To understand the origins of logistic regression, we must journey back to the early 1920s,
when the field of statistics was, in Frank Yates’ memorable phrase, “the age of correlation and
coefficients of all kinds” [20]. By 1920, statisticians had accumulated a bewildering array of
techniques—least squares, method of moments, inverse probability, the χ2 test, Pearson’s system
of curves—but lacked what Yudi Pawitan calls “a firm logical foundation.” As Dermot Turing [24]
observed, “The only orthodox way to measure probabilities was to take samples and observe their
distribution, in the manner leading to the good old Gaussian Error Function.”
    Into this confusion stepped Ronald A. Fisher2 with a revolutionary idea that would transform
not just statistics, but eventually machine learning, artificial intelligence, and the vast majority
of probabilistic learning algorithms. His method of maximum likelihood, crystallized in the
landmark 1922 paper “On the Mathematical Foundations of Theoretical Statistics” [11], provided
the missing foundation. What Fisher could not have foreseen was that this same principle would,
decades later, power everything from credit card approvals and spam filters to medical diagnoses
and large language models like Claude and ChatGPT—all trained via maximum likelihood to
estimate probabilities.
    The path from Fisher’s theoretical breakthrough to today’s ubiquitous logistic regression winds
through three distinct phases: the establishment of maximum likelihood as the gold standard
for parameter estimation, the development of probit analysis for dose-response studies, and
finally Joseph Berkson’s elegant simplification that gave us the logistic function. Each phase
built upon the previous, creating a mathematical framework that bridges classical statistics and
contemporary machine learning.

2.1     Maximum Likelihood: Fisher’s Revolutionary Framework
The Statistical Landscape of the 1920s
In the 1920s, the dominant approach in the emerging field of statistics was what we now call
“inverse probability”—essentially Bayes’ theorem applied to parameter estimation. This method
had fallen into disrepute due to philosophical objections about prior probabilities.
    Into this theoretical void came Fisher’s insight, which he had been developing since his early
1912 paper On an Absolute Criterion for Fitting Frequency Curves [9]: instead of asking “What is the
probability that the parameter equals θ given the data?” (the inverse probability approach), we
should ask “What is the probability of observing this data given that the parameter equals θ?”
(the likelihood approach). This seemingly subtle shift revolutionized statistical inference.
    Fisher’s influence would prove so profound that, as I.J. Good [13] later noted, “In 1950 the
Bayesian approach was unpopular, largely because of Fisher’s influence.” Even Harold Jeffreys,
one of the founders of modern Bayesian statistics, acknowledged Fisher’s contribution in his
influential Theory of Probability [15], describing likelihood as “a convenient term introduced by
Professor R. A. Fisher.”

Fisher’s 1922 Breakthrough: The Binomial Example
Much like Alan Turing transformed probabilistic inference into manageable arithmetic by
working with log-odds, Fisher revolutionized parameter estimation by working with the log-
likelihood. Taking logarithms converted products of probabilities into sums, making both the
calculations tractable and the optimization problem clearer.
    In his 1922 paper [11], Fisher illustrated maximum likelihood with the simplest possible
case—one that would later become the foundation of logistic regression. He wrote:
       “This question naturally first arose, not with respect to populations distributed in
       frequency curves and surfaces, but with respect to a population regarded as divided
       into two classes only, in fact in problems of probability. A certain proportion, p,
       of an infinite population is supposed to be of a certain kind, e.g., ‘successes,’ the
       remainder are then ‘failures.’ A sample of n is taken and found to contain x successes
       and y failures.”

2.2      Why Logits Over Probits?
While Fisher was developing maximum likelihood theory, applied researchers faced a series
of pressing practical problems. In the 1930s, the agricultural and pharmaceutical industries
desperately needed to understand dose-response relationships: How much insecticide kills pests?
What drug dosage is effective? How do we measure toxicity?
    The challenge was that simple linear relationships rarely worked. Researchers observed S-
shaped curves where low doses had little effect, middle doses showed rapid increases in response,

and high doses approached 100% response. The question was: how do you fit a mathematical
curve to this pattern?
   Chester Ittner Bliss, working under Fisher’s guidance at the Galton Laboratory, tackled this
problem head-on. As Bliss himself wrote [2], the goal was clear:
      “The fitting of a dosage-mortality curve to a series of experimental observations,
      however crude or refined the technique, is an attempt to infer, from a limited number
      of individuals, the ‘true’ empirical relationship of dosage and mortality for a given
      toxic agent in an infinitely larger population from which they represent only a
      sample.”
    Bliss’s breakthrough was to recognize that if individual tolerance levels followed a normal
distribution, then the cumulative response would follow the cumulative normal curve. He called
his transformed units “probits” (probability units) and showed that:

                             probit(p) = Φ−1 (p) = α + β log(dose)                                (2.5)
     This transformation converted the S-shaped dose-response curve into a straight line, making
it easy to fit using standard regression techniques. As Bliss [3] noted, this approach offered “greater
ease in determining the straight line of best fit by the simple regression equation.”
     The method was revolutionary for the time because it solved the curve-fitting problem that
had plagued researchers for decades. By 1940, probit analysis had become the standard method
for analyzing binary response data in biology, agriculture, and medicine.
     But there was a problem: the calculations were extraordinarily tedious. Fitting probit models
required looking up values in normal distribution tables, iterative calculations by hand, and
complex adjustments for edge cases. As D.J. Finney [7] noted, Fisher’s adjustments for zero
response rates in his appendix to Bliss’s paper [10] “must have mystified many mathematicophobic
biologists!”
     The computational burden was so severe that the probit method, introduced by C.I. Bliss in
the 1930s, originally defined probits with 5 added to avoid negative values when using lookup
tables. D.J. Finney’s influential 1947 text Probit Analysis [8] adopted this convention, cementing it
as the standard approach. As Collett [5] later explained: “The original definition of a probit [with
5 added] was primarily to avoid having to work with negative probits... This definition is still
used in some quarters, but in the major statistical software packages for what is referred to as
probit analysis, probits are defined without the addition of 5.” This seemingly minor adjustment
reveals just how cumbersome hand calculations had become.
     Into this computational challenge stepped Joseph Berkson with a simple but radical proposal:
what if we used a different S-shaped curve that was mathematically simpler?
     In his 1944 paper “Application of the Logistic Function to Bio-assay,” [1] Berkson proposed
adapting the logistic function for statistical analysis:

                                                    1 + e−(α+βx)

    Berkson’s motivation was refreshingly practical. As he wrote [1]:
      “I believe that the work of fitting the logistic as given here is considerably simpler than
      that of fitting the normal curve by probits and maximum likelihood as advocated by
      Bliss and Fisher.”
    Berkson identified three key advantages of the logistic function that would ultimately make
it dominant:

    • Computational Simplicity: The logistic function has a simple closed-form derivative,
      eliminating the need for complex normal distribution tables and iterative approximations.
    • Equivalent Performance: When Berkson tested both methods on real data, he found that
      “either the results are practically the same, or there is an advantage in favor of the logistic.”

foundation of the computer age. When digital computers emerged in the 1960s and entered such
areas as natural language processing in the late 1970s [21], the computational advantages of the
logistic function made it the natural choice for probabilistic modeling.
From Parameter Estimation to Loss Functions
What Fisher discovered was more profound than just a new parameter estimation method—he
had identified the fundamental principle underlying all learning from data.
From Fisher’s Binomial Likelihood to Modern Machine Learning

see that this is mathematically equivalent to minimizing negative log-likelihood. For his simple
case:

   is the same as:

                               Minimize: − x log p − y log(1 − p)                                   (2.8)

Log Loss in Modern Machine Learning

Log loss penalizes confident wrong predictions exponentially more than uncertain ones. For
example, a model that predicts a probability of 0.19 for the positive class (Y = 1) receives a loss
of 1.66, while predicting 0.79 yields a much smaller log loss of 0.23. The same principle applies
symmetrically to the negative class (Y = 0); here, we focus on Y = 1 for illustration.

Weight of Evidence (WOE) as Centered Logistic Regression
Unlike standard logistic regression with one-hot encoded categories, which measures effects
relative to a reference category, WOE recenters the model around the overall sample log-odds6 .
Instead of asking “how much higher/lower than the reference group?”, WOE asks “how much
higher/lower than the population average default rate?”.

mathematical equivalence. Using our dataset (80 defaults out of 180 high delinquency, 20 defaults
out of 520 low delinquency), we can calculate the needed components to prove the equivalence of

2.4     Solving Logistic Regression with Iterative Optimization
The previous section demonstrated a remarkable property: when using Weight of Evidence (WOE)
transformations with discretized features, we can obtain closed-form solutions for maximum
likelihood estimates. This analytical tractability is one of WOE’s key advantages in credit scoring
applications.
    However, this convenience is the exception rather than the rule. In real-world problems with
multiple predictors, continuous variables, or complex feature interactions, closed-form solutions
vanish. We must instead rely on iterative optimization algorithms—the computational foundation
underlying modern logistic regression implementations. Having established both the analytical
and computational approaches to logistic regression, we now turn to a practical application that
leverages these techniques: credit score calibration.

The Gradient Descent Approach
The logistic function’s non-linearity in its parameters means that, except for special cases such
as binary predictors with grouped data, we cannot solve for maximum likelihood estimates in
closed form. Instead, logistic regression relies on iterative optimization methods that follow the
gradient of the log-likelihood to find the parameter values that maximize it.
    To see how this works in practice, consider a simulated dataset with 200 loan applications
where we calibrate the internal bank probability of default (PD) as a function of a credit bureau
score expressed in log-odds form—that is, the log of the external PD. This setup reflects a common
scenario in which internal risk drivers are unavailable or limited, so an external PD estimate is
recalibrated to align with a lender’s internal default experience.
    This model formulation operates naturally in the log-odds space—the canonical scale of logistic
regression. The functional form of the calibration model is as follows:

     • Intercept: β0 = −2.0 (log-odds of default at the reference score)

     • Slope: β1 = 1.5 (increase in log-odds of default for each one-unit increase in score)

    Because higher probabilities of default correspond to higher risk, β1 is positive. At the

reference score (X = 0), the default probability is 1+e−2.0 ≈ 0.12 (12%). Each one-unit increase

will iteratively adjust the parameters to incorporate the observed patterns in the data, moving
toward the maximum likelihood estimates.
    The gradient descent algorithm follows a straightforward iterative logic: compute predictions
using the current parameters, measure the discrepancy between predictions and observations,
and update the parameters to reduce this discrepancy. Each iteration moves the parameters in the
direction that most improves the fit to the data, as measured by the log-likelihood. The process
continues until the parameters stabilize at values that maximize the likelihood of observing the
data. The mechanics of this procedure unfold in three steps.

  A note on Weight of Evidence (WOE) Logistic Regression

  A combination of Weight of Evidence (WOE) and Logistic Regression became a staple in
  credit risk modeling. Yet, most applied practitioners view Weight of Evidence (WOE) as
  merely a feature encoding step, with logistic regression doing the “real” work. But this is a
  misconception: WOE transformations capture predictive power through likelihood ratios,
  while logistic regression adjusts for dependencies among features.
  The mathematical reality: Under the naive Bayes assumption (features are independent
  given the class), WOE values can be seen as stand-alone univariate logistic regression
  models:
                                                       p
                          P (Y = 1|X)       P (Y = 1) X
                      log             = log          +    WOEj (xj )                                   (2.44)
                          P (Y = 0|X)       P (Y = 0) j=1
                                 p(x |Y =1)
  Each WOEj (xj ) = log p(xjj |Y =0) is a likelihood ratio that quantifies how much evidence
  feature j provides for class 1 versus class 0a .
  Yet, in practice, features are often correlated. Spiegelhalter and Knill-Jones [22] found
  that in medical diagnostics, symptoms and test results are often correlated, leading to
  overconfident predictions when using raw WOE sums. Their solution was to use WOE
  values as inputs to logistic regression:
                                                    p
                                P (Y = 1|X)        X
                            log             = β0 +     βj × WOEj (xj )                                 (2.45)
                                P (Y = 0|X)        j=1

  Here, the coefficients βj serve as adjustment factors that account for feature dependencies.
  If features were truly independent, we would expect βj ≈ 1 for all j. Deviations from 1
  indicate the degree to which the naive independence assumption is violated. In essence,
  WOE captures each feature’s univariate predictive signal through likelihood ratios, while
  logistic regression recalibrates these signals to prevent double-counting correlated evidence.
  An ensemble perspective: Each WOE value corresponds to a univariate logistic regression
  that captures the relationship between one predictor and the outcome, expressed in log-
  odds space. The final logistic regression then acts as a meta-learner that combines these
  univariate predictions:

                        Feature           Univariate model          Meta-learner
                                          WOE1 , . . . , WOEp    β0 + j βj WOEj
                                                                     P
                       x1 , . . . , x p

  Each univariate model contributes its "vote" (the WOE value), and the meta-learner
  (logistic regression) learns how much weight to give each vote, accounting for redundancy
  when features are correlated. This is why the method remains competitive with modern
  machine learning: it effectively combines strong univariate feature engineering with flexible
  multivariate calibration.

2.5      From Binary to Multinomial Logistic Regression
Up to this point, we have focused on binary outcomes—events that can occur or not occur, such as
default vs. no default or fraud vs. no fraud. Logistic regression in this setting models the log-odds
of one outcome relative to the other.
     However, many real-world problems involve more than two possible outcomes: customers
may be classified into a number of segments, transactions may belong to several fraud types—such
as first-party fraud or account takeover—and a loan may end up in one of several delinquency
states—a topic central to collections scoring.
     A note on Ordinal Outcomes

     When outcomes have a natural ordering (e.g., credit ratings from AAA to D, or delinquency
     buckets like current, 30+ days past due, 60+ days past due, 90+ days past due), ordinal logistic
     regression models can be more appropriate than standard multinomial models. These models,
     particularly common in low-default portfolio modeling, respect the ordering by modeling
     cumulative probabilities rather than treating categories as unordered. While we focus on
     multinomial logistic regression in this chapter, the fundamental principles—maximum
     likelihood estimation, log-odds transformations, and the connection to WOE—extend
     naturally to ordinal models.

    Beyond credit risk and finance, the same principle underlies state-of-the-art machine learning
systems. For instance, large language models predict the probability distribution over thousands
of possible next tokens, which is an inherently multinomial classification problem. The logic of
logistic regression extends naturally to these settings.
    This generalization from the binomial to the multinomial was already implicit in early
probability theory. Ronald Fisher, with his characteristic insight, recognized that the binomial
distribution—formalized by Jacob Bernoulli and studied for over two centuries—was merely a
special case of a broader family: the multinomial.
    As Fisher [11] wrote with characteristic precision:
        “The binomial distribution... was found by Bernoulli... that if the probability of
        an event occurring were p and the probability of it not occurring were q = 1 − p,
        then if a random sample of n trials were taken, the frequencies with which the event
        occurred 0, 1, 2, ..., n times were given by the expansion of the binomial (q + p)n .
        This rule is a particular case of a more general theorem dealing with cases in which
        not only a simple alternative is considered, but in which the event may happen in s
        ways with probabilities p1 , p2 , . . . , ps ."
    This insight—recognizing the binomial as a special case of the multinomial distribution—
directly motivates the extension from binary logistic regression to multinomial logistic regression.

    This concludes our investigation into the extension of logistic regression from binary to
multinomial settings. We have seen that the same fundamental ideas—log-odds, likelihood ratios,
and weight of evidence—extend seamlessly when the outcome involves multiple categories. The
softmax formulation generalizes the logistic link, the maximum likelihood framework remains
unchanged, and the familiar interpretability of odds and log-odds carries through to higher
dimensions. Whether implemented through the K − 1 baseline form or the full softmax
representation, the multinomial model preserves the probabilistic rigor and transparency that
make logistic regression such a cornerstone of statistical learning.
    The Weight of Evidence formulation further demonstrates how these principles can be
expressed in an intuitive and interpretable way, bridging traditional credit risk modeling with
modern machine learning algorithms. In the chapters that follow, we will build upon these
foundations to explore regularization, calibration, and practical implementation strategies for
large-scale predictive modeling.

2.6     Logistic Regression Inference: Quantifying Uncertainty
Having established the foundations of logistic regression from binary to multinomial cases, we
now turn to a critical practical question: how do we quantify the uncertainty in our predictions?
   Consider a credit risk model that predicts a 15% probability of default for a loan applicant.
Should we treat this as a precise estimate, or could the true probability be anywhere from 10% to
20%? This distinction matters enormously: regulatory capital requirements, pricing decisions,
and risk management strategies all depend on understanding not just the point estimate, but the
range of plausible values.

 Interpreting Coverage for Probability Intervals
 Before we proceed to the discussion of evaluating predictive intervals for probabilities, it is
 important to clarify what “coverage” means.
 A probability prediction interval such as [p̂L , p̂U ] is not meant to contain the binary outcome
 yi ∈ {0, 1}, but rather to provide valid uncertainty quantification for the true conditional
 probability p(xi ) = Pr(Y = 1 | X = xi ). Since individual-level true probabilities are
 unobservable, coverage is assessed through empirical event rates:
    • Binary coverage — the proportion of cases where yi ∈ [p̂L , p̂U ] — is usually near 0% or
      100%, depending on whether intervals are narrow or span most of [0, 1]. It is not an
      appropriate metric for probability intervals.
      • Conditional coverage — the key validity criterion — requires that within groups
        of similar predictions, the observed event rate falls within the predicted interval
        approximately (1 − α) of the time. Formally: Pr(p(X) ∈ [p̂L (X), p̂U (X)] | X ∈
        S) ≥ 1 − α for subsets S of the feature space.
      • Marginal coverage — a weaker but still valid guarantee — requires that averaged over
        the test distribution, intervals contain true probabilities with frequency (1 − α):
        Pr(p(X) ∈ [p̂L (X), p̂U (X)]) ≥ 1 − α.
 Note on standard conformal prediction: Classical conformal prediction for binary classifica-
 tion produces prediction sets such as {“default”}, {“not_default”}, or {“default”, “not_default”}
 with coverage guarantees for the true class label. In credit risk, however, we need probability
 intervals that quantify uncertainty in p̂i itself, not just class membership. This requires
 conformalized approaches that adapt the conformal framework to continuous probability
 estimates. Intuitively, valid coverage for probability intervals answers:
       “When I predict uncertainty bounds [p̂L , p̂U ] for groups of similar cases, how often does
        the observed event rate in those groups fall inside my stated interval?”
 This distinction explains why properly calibrated methods can yield wide intervals with zero
 binary coverage yet still provide valid uncertainty quantification—they are calibrated to cover
 true underlying probabilities, not discrete outcomes.

    While logistic regression provides point estimates of probabilities, real-world applica-
tions—particularly in credit risk and regulatory compliance—require understanding the confi-
dence we can place in these estimates. This section introduces two complementary approaches
to quantifying this uncertainty:

     • Wald-type confidence intervals: The classical approach, derived from the estimated
       covariance matrix of the coefficients. This method leverages the asymptotic normality of
       maximum likelihood estimators [14].

     • Conformalized prediction: A modern, distribution-free approach that uses empirical
       residuals to construct probability intervals with guaranteed coverage properties.

    Why two approaches? Wald intervals are computationally efficient and widely implemented,
but rely on asymptotic assumptions that may not hold in small samples. Conformalized prediction
2.6. LOGISTIC REGRESSION INFERENCE: QUANTIFYING UNCERTAINTY                                     49


offers finite-sample validity without parametric assumptions, at the cost of requiring a separate
calibration dataset. Understanding both methods equips practitioners to choose the right tool
for their specific context.

of the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs). Under regularity conditions, the estimated
coefficients βb are approximately normal with covariance matrix equal to the inverse of the
observed information matrix.

their estimated values, with uncertainty captured by the curvature of the likelihood surface.
A sharply peaked likelihood (high curvature) indicates we have strong information about the
parameters, leading to narrow confidence intervals. A flat likelihood (low curvature) indicates
greater uncertainty and wider intervals.

prediction provides an alternative, distribution-free approach. Rather than relying on theoretical
assumptions, it uses empirical nonconformity scores11 from a calibration dataset to construct
probability intervals that achieve valid coverage in finite samples.
    The fundamental shift: Instead of asking "what does theory tell us about parameter uncer-
tainty?", conformalized prediction asks "how wrong was our model on past data, and how can
we use that to quantify future uncertainty?" This shifts the focus from parametric inference to
data-driven calibration of uncertainty.

2.7     Later Developments: From Statistics to Machine Learning
By the 1970s, logistic regression had established itself as the standard approach for binary
classification in statistics. What followed was a remarkable transformation: the migration
of these ideas from statistical inference to computational learning, and ultimately to the neural
networks that dominate modern machine learning.
The Computational Revolution (1970s-1980s)
The 1970s marked a turning point where logistic regression transitioned from a statistical
technique requiring manual calculation to a computational tool. Two developments were
particularly significant:
    Generalized Linear Models (1972): Nelder and Wedderburn [19] introduced the GLM
framework, unifying logistic regression with other models through the concept of link functions.
This abstraction revealed that logistic regression was not an isolated technique but part of a
broader mathematical structure. The GLM framework provided efficient iteratively reweighted
least squares (IRLS) algorithms that made fitting logistic models computationally tractable.
    Discrete Choice Theory: Daniel McFadden’s work on multinomial logistic regression and
discrete choice analysis [18] extended logistic regression beyond binary outcomes. His theoretical
contributions, which earned him the Nobel Prize in Economics (2000), showed how the logistic
function naturally emerges from assumptions about random utility maximization. This connected
statistical modeling to economic theory and opened new applications in transportation, marketing,
and policy analysis.
54                                                                                        References


The Machine Learning Era (1980s-2000s)
As computing power increased, logistic regression found new life in machine learning, where the
focus shifted from inference to prediction:
    Regularization Methods: The introduction of ridge regression and later the LASSO (Least Ab-
solute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) in the 1990s addressed overfitting in high-dimensional
settings. Regularized logistic regression became essential for handling datasets where the number
of features approached or exceeded the number of observations—a common scenario in genomics,
text classification, and early web applications.
    Ensemble Methods: The 2000s saw logistic regression incorporated into ensemble frameworks.
Boosting algorithms like AdaBoost and later gradient boosting machines combined multiple weak
learners (often decision stumps) with logistic loss functions. This marriage of tree-based methods
and logistic regression produced some of the most successful predictive models in practice.
    Large-Scale Applications: The rise of internet companies in the late 1990s and 2000s created
unprecedented demand for scalable classification algorithms. Logistic regression, with its convex
loss function and efficient optimization, became the workhorse of spam filtering, ad click
prediction, and recommendation systems. Companies like Google and Facebook deployed logistic
regression models on billions of data points, driving innovations in distributed computing.
Contemporary Perspectives
Today, when you apply for a credit card and receive an instant decision, when your email filters
spam with uncanny accuracy, or when a medical algorithm helps diagnose disease, you’re witness-
ing the culmination of this remarkable journey. From Fisher’s maximum likelihood framework to
Berkson’s logistic simplification, from Cox’s mathematical refinements to McFadden’s economic
applications, these contributions have evolved into the algorithms that power modern decisioning.
    The logistic function—an “underdog” once dismissed as inferior to its probit rival—now
powers the decision engines of the modern economy. In the end, Berkson’s vision proved prophetic:
sometimes the “inferior” alternative becomes the foundation upon which entire multi-billion
industries are built.
