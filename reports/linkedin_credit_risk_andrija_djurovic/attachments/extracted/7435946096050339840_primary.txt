Internal Ratings-Based Modeling Process

   The development of an Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) model consists of two main areas: the development
   of the risk differentiation function and risk quantification.

   Typically, these two steps are performed on different datasets, with significantly greater flexibility left to
   practitioners in the development of the risk differentiation function.

   Development of the risk differentiation function is the first step in the overall model development
   process. The main goal of this step is to ensure the model can adequately rank risk, providing
   meaningful differentiation between low- and high-risk obligors or facilities.

   After developing the risk differentiation function, practitioners perform the risk quantification process,
   which essentially involves assigning an appropriate risk level to the output of the risk differentiation
   function.

   In practice, there is often a difference between the risk quantification process for continuous and discrete
   outputs of the risk differentiation function.

   Regardless of the type of output (continuous or discrete) of the risk differentiation function, risk
   quantification typically consists of specific steps such as: assignment of the base or best estimate,
   quantification of appropriate adjustments (AA), quantification of the margin of conservatism (MoC), and
   quantification of any other additional conservatism, for instance that required by certain local regulators.

   These steps apply to all three risk parameters: Probability of Default (PD), Loss Given Default (LGD),
   and Exposure at Default (EAD).

Probability of Default Modeling in Practice

    As with other IRB models, the first step in PD model development is typically the creation of the risk
    differentiation function. The main goal of this step is to ensure sufficient ranking ability, providing
    meaningful risk differentiation between obligors or facilities.

    Typically, after developing and adopting the final risk differentiation function, practitioners discretize the
    PD model output into rating scale. In the case of logistic regression, the most common approach is to
    discretize the model’s log-odds. While doing so, practitioners often ensure specific characteristics of the
    rating scale, such as monotonicity, heterogeneity, homogeneity, and lack of concentration within rating
    grades.

    After creating the rating scale, practitioners calibrate the model, usually at the level of the rating grade.
    The main purpose of this step is to assign an appropriate risk level to each rating grade.

    It is worth noting that the datasets used for developing the risk differentiation function and for
    calibration often differ. Thus, calibration represents an important and regulatory-required step in IRB PD
    model development.

    The calibration process includes several steps, from assigning the so-called best or base estimate to
    finally adding a certain level of conservatism.

    Depending on local regulatory requirements, different types of additional conservatism may be
    incorporated as part of the final estimate.

Probability of Default Modeling in Practice cont.


    The first step of the calibration process is estimating the best (base) estimates, which, in most cases, is
    the average default rate per rating grade calculated using the full calibration dataset. These estimated
    averages per rating grade are usually referred to as long-run averages.

    The regulatory prescribed method for calculating long-run averages is the arithmetic average of yearly
    default rates; however, regulations also allow weighted averages in specific cases.

    When it is not possible to assign ratings to the full calibration dataset, practitioners often calibrate the
    rating scale by matching the central tendency at the portfolio level, starting from the best estimates at
    the rating level derived from the development dataset.

    This presentation focuses on the scenario in which it is possible to assign a rating to each observation in
    the calibration dataset, allowing calculation of the best estimate at the level of the rating grade.
    However, instead of the common practice of assigning long-run averages to rating grades and losing the
    link with the raw model estimates, this presentation introduces a model-based calibration of the rating
    scale, with details provided in the following slides.

Model-Based Rating Scale Calibration


   By calculating best estimates as long-run averages of default rates per rating grade, practitioners lose the
   link between the model output and the final estimates.

   Moreover, although the final model output is a rating scale and each obligor or facility within the same
   rating grade receives the same estimate, model development typically indicates that there are differences
   in the estimated PD even within a single rating grade.

   Therefore, maintaining a link between the raw model outputs and calibrated best estimates can be
   beneficial and can serve as a critical input for further analyses of model structure quality.

   Some advantages of model-based calibration include:

       1 Model outputs calibrated in this way can serve as a key input for statistical testing of
         homogeneity in IRB models.
       2 It enables continuous monitoring of omitted risk factor bias.
       3 It allows for improved residual analysis and the potential identification of segments where the
         model over- or under-predicts observed default rates.

Model-Based Rating Scale Calibration cont.


   However, when using calibrated values from model-based calibration, practitioners should be mindful of
   potential changes in the ranking order of individual estimates across rating grades. Although the ranking
   order is preserved within a rating grade, disruptions in the ranking of some obligors or facilities across
   rating grades may indicate a lack of model heterogeneity between grades or significant differences
   between the training sample and the calibration sample. Therefore, practitioners are encouraged to think
   critically about how the calibrated estimates are used in each analysis.

   The following slides illustrate model-based calibration by optimizing the intercept in a logistic regression
   model, using the raw model’s log-odds as an offset in the regression.
   As this example is for illustrative purposes, practitioners are encouraged to explore alternative approaches
   and compare calibration quality.

   Additionally, practitioners should be aware that the illustrated example refers to default-weighted
   calibration, which, in certain cases is not a regulatory-compliant method. However, a straightforward
   extension of the same idea to the arithmetic average of yearly default rates can be achieved by applying
   frequency weights within the model estimation process.

As shown in the calibration dataset summary, the average PDs per rating grade (avg_pd) do not match the average

mentioned earlier, these estimates are referred to as best or base estimates.
Although not shown in the table, calculating the weighted average of the PDs and observed default rates does not
