PD Model Development


   Probability of Default (PD) modeling consists of a few standardized steps. Although the
   process can differ significantly within specific steps, the overall modeling approach can be
   considered standardized, following certain principles.
   After collecting data, one of the first steps in PD modeling is to develop the risk
   differentiation function.
   Regardless of whether the model is used for regulatory or managerial purposes, this step is
   equally important and is often based on the same or similar principles.
   The main goal of the risk differentiation function is to ensure that the model can
   adequately rank risk, providing meaningful differentiation between low- and high-risk
   obligors or facilities.

   Typical steps in the development of the risk differentiation function include single-factor
   analysis, risk factor engineering, correlation analysis, and multi-factor analysis. These steps
   may overlap in certain areas and can, in essence, be considered integral parts of the risk
   differentiation function development, along with additional sub-steps within them.

   One of the most commonly used statistical methods in multi-factor analysis is logistic
   regression. This approach is typically considered the traditional method for PD model
   development.
   With the increasing use of machine learning (ML) in credit risk modeling, practitioners are
   exploring additional possibilities to enhance the development of the risk differentiation
   function using alternative methods.
   However, practitioners can still combine traditional and ML approaches while supporting the
   core principles of PD model development. One such example is risk factor engineering in
   PD modeling.
   The focus of this presentation is on risk factor engineering in PD modeling that goes
   beyond standard binning approaches. Specifically, the emphasis is on the derivation of new
   risk factors based on interactions identified by decision tree models. As the presented
   examples serve only for demonstration purposes, practitioners are encouraged to consider
   different use cases and designs for this approach.

Risk Factor Engineering in PD Modeling

   Risk factor engineering in PD modeling is one of the most important steps in the
   development of the risk differentiation function.
   In traditional PD model development, this step is often equated with the binning of risk
   factors (both numerical and categorical), although its application can extend far beyond this
   approach.
   Regardless of the final use of the PD models (regulatory or managerial), the principles
   followed in the binning step are largely shared.

   The following list presents some of the inputs and considerations typically used in a
   standard binning process:

         minimum number of observations per bin;
         minimum number of defaults (or target rate) per bin;
         treatment of the missing or special values;
         monotonic relationship between the risk factor and the target variable;
         statistical or practical significance of risk factor bin profiles;
         expert input and confirmation of the statistical relationship between the risk factor
         and the target variable.

   When practitioners consider alternative risk factor engineering methods beyond binning, the
   same or similar principles are typically incorporated into this modeling step.
   The ultimate goal of these principles is to ensure full explainability of the impact and strong
   business support for the risk factors selected in the final model.
   One approach to risk factor engineering that goes beyond the standard binning process is
   tree-based interaction extraction. In essence, this method involves deriving new risk factors
   from decision tree models, which can then be used as variables in logistic regression or even
   as challenger models. When used as a challenger model, practitioners can also utilize the
   identified risk factors as supporting analysis in the risk factor selection process.

Tree-Based Interactions
    Interactions between risk factors can be extracted from standard decision tree algorithms.
    However, standard algorithms typically do not provide all the inputs needed to support key
    considerations in risk factor engineering. For instance, requirements that are often
    unsupported or only partially supported include the minimum number of defaults or target
    rate, specific treatment of missing (and special) values, monotonicity constraints, statistical
    testing as a splitting criterion, and control over tree depth.
    Given the strong requirements for risk factor engineering in PD modeling, Andrija Djurovic
    proposed a customized tree-based approach. The main adjustments to the standard
    decision tree methodology relate to more flexible inputs:
          minimum number of observations in the final nodes;
          minimum target rate in the final nodes;
          separate treatment of numerical and categorical risk factors;
          maximum tree depth;
          statistical testing as the splitting criterion;
          monotonicity constraints at the splitting node;
          treatment of missing values at the splitting node to ensure monotonicity;
          overall flexibility for applying this approach across different modeling designs.

Splitting criterion - Gini Index

The Gini Index (or Gini impurity) is a standard splitting criterion used in decision tree algorithms
for binary target variables. It measures the degree of class mixing within a dataset, where higher
values indicate greater impurity. In this context, impurity reflects the extent to which both classes
are present within a node; the more balanced the class composition, the higher the impurity.
For the selected binary split, Gini Index is given as follows:

Adding a minus sign in the above formula turns the selection criterion into a maximization
problem; thus, the split that maximizes the expression is selected for splitting at a given node.

Treatment of Categorical and Numerical Risk Factors

In PD modeling, practitioners often work with a limited number of inherently categorical risk factors. For this
reason, these variables are rarely fully automatically engineered and directly used in the modeling process. Even
in tree-based methods, it is recommended to first examine and prepare categorical risk factors before further use.
Accordingly, the customized decision tree process treats categorical risk factors using standard inputs such as the
number of observations and target rate. However, it also applies an initial ordering based on the alphabetical
sequence of risk factor modalities. This requires ensuring a meaningful ordering of categorical modalities.

On the other hand, numerical risk factors are initially split based on a predefined number of cut points derived
from observed quantiles. Additional considerations, such as the number of observations and target rate, are also
incorporated into this process. Monotonicity constraints are enforced at each splitting node. As a result,
splitting points may be adjusted to preserve monotonicity, which is one of the key criteria in risk factor
engineering used in credit risk. Practitioners should note that monotonicity at the splitting node does not
necessarily ensure monotonicity throughout the full tree structure. However, it significantly increases the
likelihood of achieving overall monotonic behavior.

For both types of risk factors, missing values are assigned to a node in a way that preserves the monotonicity
constraint. As a result, missing value preprocessing in this sense is not needed.

In addition to these criteria, the customized algorithm can control overall tree depth as well as the p-value used
in the two-sample test. This reduces the need for additional tree pruning, which is often required when other
splitting metrics are used.

Simulation Study

The purpose of this simulation is to demonstrate one use case of tree-based interaction extraction
as a support for standard PD model development. Specifically, three numerical risk factors
(maturity, amount, age) are used as inputs to the customized decision tree algorithm. The
output of this step is then used as an additional risk factor. The final PD model is specified in the
form default ~ balance + rf.inter, where rf.inter denotes a variable extracted from the
decision tree, with Weights of Evidence (WoE) encoding applied to the risk factor modalities.

The customized decision tree is run under the following setup:
   1   the minimum percentage of observations in the final node is set to 10%;
   2   the minimum percentage of defaults in the final node is set to 5%;
   3   the monotonicity constraint in the splitting node is activated;
   4   the maximum number of splitting points for all numerical variables is set to 50 at each
       splitting node;
   5   the p-value from the two-sample test of proportions is used as the splitting criterion;
   6   the p-value threshold is set at the 5% significance level, supporting the tree growing process.

Conclusions

   One possible use case of ML in PD modeling is to support the risk factor engineering process.

   Extracting risk factor interactions from tree-based models can provide additional benefits to the
   traditional PD modeling approach. Beyond the typical improvement in discriminatory power, this
   approach can also enhance the robustness of the final model by selecting a broader range of relevant
   risk factors.

   The same use case of tree-based interactions can be easily extended to Loss Given Default (LGD) and
   Exposure at Default (EAD) models, where practitioners often face greater challenges in achieving the
   required model performance.

   In addition to the presented use case, the same idea can be extended to a modular approach to model
   development, different designs for combining risk factors, as well as other statistical methods such as
   random forests.

   Practitioners should be aware that once interactions are extracted and used as inputs in a regression
   model, the investigation of the risk differentiation function is not complete. Further critical steps
   include analyzing the relationship between the individual risk factors (those used to construct the
   interaction) and the target variable. This analysis is essential to properly assess and validate the final
   risk differentiation function from a business perspective.

   A model developed using this approach can serve as a challenger model, allowing practitioners to
   identify relevant risk factors, their interactions, and nonlinear relationships, while also using the tree
   structure to improve the binning step in traditional model development.
