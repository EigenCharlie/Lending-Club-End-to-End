Discriminatory Power in IRB Modeling


   Testing discriminatory power is one of the key aspects of model assessment conducted by credit risk
   practitioners.

   In simple terms, within the IRB framework, discriminatory power testing evaluates whether a model
   provides meaningful differentiation between facilities or obligors based on the levels of risk parameters
   assigned and the values observed in the validation sample.

   Somers’ D is commonly used to assess discriminatory power in Loss Given Default (LGD) and Exposure
   and Default (EAD) models because the final outputs of these risk parameters are typically ordinal, with a
   limited number of distinct values. In practice, this often corresponds to rating grades or pools.

   When calculating Somers’ D, practitioners frequently adopt the ECB approach presented in the
   Instructions for Reporting the Validation Results of Internal Models.

   In their instructions, the ECB differentiates between models that output 20 or fewer and more than 20
   unique values.

   For 20 or fewer unique values, practitioners often adopt model-implied thresholds to discretize the
   realised parameter values as a preparatory step for calculating Somers’ D.

   Moreover, most practitioners interpret the requested risk parameter estimate from the ECB instructions
   as the one used for the calculation of own funds requirements. In other words, these are the final model
   estimates which, in the case of LGD performing, LGD in-default, and EAD models, are
   downturn-adjusted and include all margins of conservatism that have been added.

   Given this interpretation of the risk parameter estimate and the fact that model-implied thresholds are
   used for Somers’ D calculation, it is worth investigating the impact of conservatism on conclusions about
   the model’s discriminatory power. Specifically, how changes in estimates during the risk quantification
   process affect discriminatory power as measured by Somers’ D.

   This presentation examines the impact of conservatism added to the model’s best estimate on overall
   discriminatory power, given the rating grades of the model output. Practitioners should keep in mind
   that the relationships between Somers’ D measures shown in this simulation demonstrate a simplified
   framework, assuming equal conservatism across rating pools. Still, this work can serve as a basis for
   developing an appropriate method for determining what an LGD or EAD model should actually
   discriminate.

   The following slides first introduce the basic concept of Somers’ D used to measure discriminatory power
   in LGD and EAD models, and then investigate the relationship between Somers’ D and the conservatism
   added to the model’s best estimate.

Somers’ D in LGD and EAD Modeling


In LGD and EAD modeling, Somers’ D has been adopted as a standard metric for measuring a model’s
discriminatory power.

Given that Somers’ D is inherently a pair of asymmetric coefficients, for a contingency table (X , Y ), Dxy is
considered an appropriate measure of the performance of X in predicting or ranking Y .

The formula for calculating the Somers’ Dxy coefficient for a contingency table (X , Y ) is:

                                                         P−Q
                                              Dxy =          P
                                                      n2 −       (nj )2

where:

         P is twice the number of concordant pairs;
         Q is twice the number of discordant pairs;
         n is the total number of observations in the contingency table;
         nj denotes the column marginal totals.

   As for the calculation of Somers’ D presented in the ECB instructions, realised risk parameters need to
   be discretized.

   For models with 20 or fewer unique outputs, model-implied thresholds are used to discretize the realised
   risk parameter values.

   However, in practice, different approaches are observed when preparing inputs for the calculation of
   Somers’ D in LGD and EAD modeling, and there are no standard guidelines on how to approach this task.

   Details on the impact of the binning approach on a model’s discriminatory power have already been
   investigated by Andrija Djurovic, and part of the simulation results can be found here.

   For the purpose of this presentation, the ECB approach has been adopted. According to the ECB
   instructions (page 32), for a model that outputs two facility grades, F1 and F2, realised LGD is
   segmented into three classes. The first class comprises all realised LGD values that are less than or equal
   to the estimated LGD of grade F1. The second class comprises all realised LGD values that are less than
   or equal to the estimated LGD of grade F2 and are not part of the first class. The third class comprises
   all realized LGD values that are greater than the estimated LGD of grade F2.

Simulation Study Design
The purpose of this simulation is to examine the impact of conservatism added to the model’s best estimate on
the Somers’ D value, under the assumption that the final model output consists of five rating pools and with
equivalent conservatism across grades.

The data used in this simulation are available at the following link, with LGD_R representing realised LGD values
and LGD_P_pool representing the final model pools.

have been added to all pools’ best estimates, starting from 1 and ending at 25 percentage points.
