𝗕𝗮𝗰𝗸𝘁𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗣𝗗 𝗠𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗹𝘀 - 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗠𝘂𝗹𝘁𝗶-𝗣𝗲𝗿𝗶𝗼𝗱 𝗔𝘃𝗲𝗿𝗮𝗴𝗲 𝗧𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴
In the context of Probability of Default (PD) IRB modeling, backtesting is one of the most comprehensive exercises conducted across different stages of the modeling process. The primary purpose of backtesting is to ensure that model estimates accurately predict the occurrence of defaults, i.e., that PD estimates provide reliable forecasts of default rates. Given the nature of these estimates, average PDs are typically backtested over a recent period of three to five years across different subsegments using the z-score test.
When preparing inputs for multi-period average testing, practitioners often overlook the fact that the estimated PD is calculated as an arithmetic mean of yearly default rates. Therefore, the preparation of inputs must ensure that the observed default rates are properly aligned with the estimated PDs.
In my latest presentation, I compare two commonly used methods for preparing inputs for multi-period average testing with a method that fully aligns with the calibration process. As demonstrated by the simulation results, these approaches lead to different test outcomes. Moreover, only the approach that fully aligns with the calibration process is statistically valid.
In some of my upcoming posts, I will compare the statistical validity of these different approaches, confirming the appropriateness of only the method proposed in this presentation. I will also demonstrate how the dependency structure can be incorporated into the testing procedure.
Given that the only statistically valid approach is the one aligned with the PD calibration process, where long-run average default rates are calculated as arithmetic averages of yearly default rates, practitioners are encouraged to consider how this impacts other aspects of PD model validation involving the use of multi-period observations, particularly aspects related to model heterogeneity and homogeneity.
#irb #pd #backtesting #zscoretest

𝗥𝗶𝘀𝗸 𝗙𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗼𝗿 𝗘𝗻𝗴𝗶𝗻𝗲𝗲𝗿𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗶𝗻 𝗣𝗗 𝗠𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗹𝘀 - 𝗧𝗿𝗲𝗲-𝗕𝗮𝘀𝗲𝗱 𝗜𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀
Risk factor engineering is a critical step in every PD model development. In traditional frameworks using logistic regression, it is often equated with the binning of individual risk factors.
With the increased use of machine learning (ML) in credit risk, practitioners are exploring additional ways to enhance the development of the risk differentiation function using alternative methods.
One ML approach to risk factor engineering that goes beyond standard binning is tree-based interaction extraction. In essence, this method involves deriving new risk factors from decision tree models, which can then be used as inputs in logistic regression or even as challenger models.
My latest presentation demonstrates a tailored approach to risk factor engineering based on decision tree models. Adjustments to standard decision tree algorithms refer to the incorporation of core binning principles typically considered in credit risk model development.
As the presented method serves illustrative purposes only, practitioners are encouraged to consider additional adjustments to the proposed algorithm that best suit their use case.
The same approach can be easily extended to LGD and EAD modeling, as well as to other designs, such as those using random forest.
In the coming months, I plan to develop an R package to facilitate the use of such methods in credit risk model development.

𝗕𝗮𝗰𝗸𝘁𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗣𝗗 𝗠𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗹𝘀 - 𝗢𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗥𝗲𝗹𝗶𝗮𝗯𝗶𝗹𝗶𝘁𝘆 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗠𝘂𝗹𝘁𝗶-𝗣𝗲𝗿𝗶𝗼𝗱 𝗡𝗼𝗿𝗺𝗮𝗹 𝗧𝗲𝘀𝘁
As risk quantification is a regulatory requirement in IRB modeling, practitioners pay significant attention to meeting these requirements. In addition, they must assess the appropriateness of model estimates - typically referred to as backtesting.
In practice, PD backtesting is one of the most comprehensive validation exercises, usually examining model estimates across different portfolio subsegments and time dimensions.
A commonly applied approach is to backtest PD estimates over a recent period, covering the latest three to five years. In some cases, this analysis is extended further back, but it still relies on a relatively limited number of observations.
One method used to assess these estimates against observed default rates is the normal test.
My latest presentation examines the reliability of the normal test given the relatively limited number of observations used for testing. More specifically, it analyzes the Type I error under three different data-generating processes for default rates, considering both independent and correlated observations over time, as well as deviations from normality.
Since multi-period backtesting of PD models is a topic practitioners often struggle with - particularly when aligning it with the standard IRB calibration process - some of my future work will focus on this area. Specifically, I will propose an alternative method for multi-period averaging tests that fully aligns with the calibration process.

𝗦𝗼𝗺𝗲𝗿𝘀’ 𝗗 𝗶𝗻 𝗟𝗚𝗗 𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗘𝗔𝗗 𝗠𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗹𝗶𝗻𝗴 - 𝗜𝗺𝗽𝗮𝗰𝘁 𝗼𝗳 𝗖𝗼𝗻𝘀𝗲𝗿𝘃𝗮𝘁𝗶𝘀𝗺 𝗼𝗻 𝗗𝗶𝘀𝗰𝗿𝗶𝗺𝗶𝗻𝗮𝘁𝗼𝗿𝘆 𝗣𝗼𝘄𝗲𝗿
Somers’ D is a commonly used metric for assessing discriminatory power in LGD and EAD models. When calculating Somers’ D, practitioners often adopt input preparation steps from the ECB approach, as presented in the Instructions for Reporting the Validation Results of Internal Models. Practitioners typically interpret the requested risk parameter estimate from the ECB instructions as the one used for calculating own funds requirements. In other words, as the final model estimates which, in the case of LGD performing, LGD in-default, and EAD models, are downturn-adjusted and include all margins of conservatism that have been added.
Given this interpretation of the ECB instructions, along with the model-implied thresholds for discretization of the realized risk parameters for models whose output is considered discrete, it is worth investigating how conservatism impacts conclusions on discriminatory power testing.
My latest presentation provides a simplified simulation framework investigating the impact of conservatism on the discriminatory power results of an LGD model, measured by Somers’ D, for a given rating grade structure.
As with earlier posts on the use of Somers’ D in LGD and EAD models, practitioners are encouraged to consider different approaches and are always advised to base their final conclusions on discriminatory power while keeping in mind the possible impact of modeling choices on the testing results.

The quantification of Margin of Conservatism (MoC) Type C related to uncertainty in estimating the long-run average (LRA) default rate is one of the key regulatory topics in IRB model development, for which various approaches are commonly observed in practice. Although the ECB, in its Guide to Internal Models, sets clear expectations regarding the sources of uncertainty that should be considered, practitioners still hold differing views on how these sources should be incorporated into the quantification process.
To properly balance the selected statistical approach with its underlying assumptions, it is critical to understand the contribution of each source to the overall MoC level.
In my latest presentation, I examine several commonly used statistical methods for quantifying this type of MoC. As shown by the simulation results, the choice of statistical approach can lead to significant differences in MoC Type C levels related to the LRA default rate. These findings raise important questions and highlight challenges practitioners face in this area.
Practitioners should note that the presented methods are not exhaustive; other approaches, as well as combinations of these methods, also exist. However, each approach relies on specific assumptions, making it essential to understand their limitations and balance them against regulatory requirements.
As a further extension of this topic, I am working on R and Python packages for the quantification of MoC Type C in PD models. I expect to release the first development version in the coming months.

𝗕𝗮𝗰𝗸𝘁𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗣𝗿𝗼𝗯𝗮𝗯𝗶𝗹𝗶𝘁𝘆 𝗼𝗳 𝗗𝗲𝗳𝗮𝘂𝗹𝘁 𝗠𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗹𝘀 - 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗧𝘄𝗼-𝗦𝗶𝗱𝗲𝗱 𝗘𝘅𝗮𝗰𝘁 𝗕𝗶𝗻𝗼𝗺𝗶𝗮𝗹 𝗧𝗲𝘀𝘁
Backtesting PD models is typically one of the most comprehensive validation exercises performed during model development and validation.
In practice, three statistical tests are commonly employed for backtesting PD estimates at the level of individual years: the exact binomial test, the z-score test, and Jeffreys’ test. The exact binomial test relies on the discrete binomial distribution of the number of defaults, while the z-score and Jeffreys’ tests rely on a continuous approximation of the binomial proportion distribution.
The z-score test is traditionally used in both one-sided and two-sided forms, whereas the exact binomial test is often perceived by practitioners as lacking a natural two-sided extension. While this may be true within the standard hypothesis testing framework, a two-sided p-value for the exact binomial test can nevertheless be calculated.
The purpose of this presentation is to demonstrate how to calculate the p-value of the two-sided exact binomial test. It first introduces the basics of the exact binomial test and its application in the backtesting of PD models, and then provides R and Python examples for calculating the p-value.
As with all tests used for backtesting PD models (including those that assume correlated defaults), the results rely on specific assumptions and align to varying degrees with the IRB framework. Practitioners are therefore encouraged to consider these aspects when using and interpreting the results.

Entire generations of credit risk modelers used IV thresholds without ever asking where they actually came from.
Most credit risk practitioners use this table almost on a daily basis.
But why exactly these thresholds and not some others?
Partly because they worked reasonably well in practice.
And partly because, over time, they became embedded into the industry’s collective intuition:
- passed from practitioner to practitioner,
- from validator to bank,
- from mentor to analyst.
The interesting part is that the mathematical foundations needed to formalize this existed long ago.
- Information theory.
- Likelihood ratios.
- Statistical divergence measures.
Credit risk modeling had access to these tools for years.
Yet much of the industry continued relying on inherited interpretation rules and rules of thumb.
I increasingly think credit risk evolved operationally faster than academically.
A surprising amount of knowledge in this field still propagates through institutional memory rather than formal statistical standardization.
Recently I came across an interesting paper attempting to statistically formalize IV significance testing.

what other famous “rules of thumb” still dominate everyday credit risk practice?

Research Insight: Are People Systematically Inactive Across Financial Decisions?
An insightful working paper from Danmarks Nationalbank (No. 216, September 2025) authored by Henrik Yde Andersen, Camilla Skovbo Christensen, Claus Thustrup Kreiner, and Søren Leth-Petersen explores an intriguing question:
Do individuals who stay inactive in one financial decision - such as not refinancing a mortgage - also tend to be inactive in others, like adjusting retirement savings?
Using Danish administrative data and quasi-experimental methods, the study finds a striking independence between financial decisions. In other words, those who remain inactive in one context are not systematically inactive in another.
This finding challenges the idea that financial inaction stems from stable personal traits (like low literacy or attention) - instead, it highlights how context-specific factors play a much larger role. The implication: financial inaction - and its costs - are broadly distributed rather than concentrated among certain groups.
A highly recommended read for anyone interested in behavioral finance, household decision-making, and economic policy design.

Is it possible to predict the FX rate movements?
At yesterdays presentation at Sveriges riksbank, Anna Seim and Olav Syrstad argued that while it is essentially not possible to beat a random walk model for predictions forward in time, it is possible to build models that explain the historical movements in terms of macro economic variables.
This might looks like a paradox, why could not the model be used for predictions? A potential answer lies in that the macro variables needed to compute the model in the backward scenario is available, while in a prediction scenario they also need to be predicted themselves. If prediction of the macro variables are as uncertain as the FX itself, the model would not really have anything to go on (as it can only relate two unknown things to each other).
A version of this effect is debated in IRRBB as well. EBA heat maps tells us that we should include (among many other things) macro variables in the behavioral models. Yet while a baseline forecast might be available for the macro variables, we have no information about how they respond to one of the Basel shocks with the exception of interest rates or inflations which can be assumed to have a strong relationship.
As such, while it looks tempting to include all variables predictive in a retrospective study, the FX case teach us that it might not help forward prediction or risk management.
Would love to hear reflections or thought on FX prediction, have you seen predictive models beating random walk?
