Linear Regression in LGD and EAD Modeling


   The development of an Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) model encompasses two primary
   areas: the creation of the risk differentiation function and risk quantification.
   A common approach to developing the risk differentiation function in Loss Given Default
   (LGD) and Exposure at Default (EAD) models is linear regression, specifically Ordinary
   Least Squares (OLS). Although linear regression is usually considered part of multi-factor
   analysis, the preceding steps of risk factor engineering should also be viewed as essential to
   properly applying linear regression in this context.
   The development of LGD and EAD risk differentiation functions typically involves both
   numeric and categorical risk factors.
   The final model typically comprises a set of categorical risk factors selected through OLS
   regression. Some of these are the result of discretizing numeric risk factors, while others are
   inherently categorical by nature.

Linear Regression in LGD and EAD Modeling cont.

   Regardless of the process used to create categorical risk factors, practitioners usually have
   an a priori expectation regarding their relationship with the modeled target. In other words,
   practitioners can typically assess whether one modality of a risk factor is more or less risky
   than another, where the level of riskiness is evaluated based on the expected or average
   value of the modeled target across the analyzed modalities. An example of such a risk
   factor is a discretized numeric variable for which practitioners have applied monotonic
   binning constraints as part of the standard IRB development process for the risk
   differentiation function.
   Given these expectations about the relationship between a risk factor and the modeled
   target, practitioners often face challenges when selecting an appropriate encoding method
   for risk factors to be used in multi-factor analysis.
   This presentation focuses on three different encoding methods often considered alongside
   linear regression models. The main goal is to demonstrate how the selection of the
   encoding method affects the interpretation of the linear regression model, emphasizing the
   importance of aligning different modeling steps in IRB development. Although the
   presented examples refer to a linear regression approach, the same principles apply to
   logistic regression models typically used in Probability of Default (PD) modeling.

Encoding Methods in Practice


   To prepare a categorical risk factor for linear regression, practitioners must select a specific
   encoding method.
   In simple terms, encoding refers to transforming categorical risk factors into one or more
   numeric variables suitable for use in linear regression models.
   Encoding is often treated as a mechanical step required to run the regression, with
   insufficient attention given to its alignment with preceding modeling steps and with the
   criteria used to select the final model within the linear regression framework.

   This presentation focuses on comparing the following three encoding methods, raising
   awareness of their interpretation and their alignment with other modeling steps:
      1   ordinal encoding;
      2   one-hot encoding;
      3   nested dummy encoding (also known as partition variables).
   Further details about the nested dummy encoding method can be found here.

Encoding Methods in Practice cont.
Ordinal Encoding Method

      This encoding method applies to categorical variables that exhibit ordinal relationships among their
      categories.
      In simple terms, it involves replacing each modality of a risk factor with a number that reflects the
      ordinal relationship between modalities. A simple example is a risk factor with two modalities, 01
      (-Inf,20.1001) and 02 [20.1001,Inf), replaced by the numbers 1 and 2, indicating different risk
      levels.
      Given the nature of ordinal encoding, the main question practitioners should consider is whether this
      method aligns with preceding modeling steps (for example, monotonic binning and the criteria applied
      there), as well as with the interpretation of the model output under the assumed changes in risk levels
      across risk factor modalities.
      Additionally, practitioners should be aware of the expected sign of the linear regression coefficient
      associated with each risk factor and verify its alignment with the anticipated relationship.


One-Hot Encoding Method

      One-hot encoding transforms a categorical risk factor with k modalities into k-1 so-called dummy
      variables.
      In simple terms, a value of 1 is assigned to observations belonging to the analyzed modality of the risk
      factor and 0 to all other observations. The resulting number of variables is always reduced by one to
      avoid perfect multicollinearity among dummy variables.
      Due to its construction, this encoding method can be applied to both nominal and ordinal risk factors.
      However, the interpretation of the estimated coefficients is always relative to the so-called reference
      modality - the one omitted during the construction of dummy variables.

Encoding Methods in Practice cont.

One-Hot Encoding Method cont.

      When using this method, practitioners should carefully interpret the p-values from linear regression and
      consider the relationships among the estimated coefficients of the dummy variables referring to the same
      risk factor.


Nested Dummy Encoding Method

      Nested dummy encoding is often considered an alternative to standard one-hot encoding, although it is
      less commonly used in credit risk modeling.
      Similar to ordinal encoding, it applies to categorical variables that exhibit ordinal relationships among
      their categories.
      Its main characteristic is the establishment of connections between adjacent categories, which makes this
      encoding method particularly attractive in credit risk modeling - especially when practitioners bin
      numeric risk factors and aim to identify statistically significant risk differences between adjacent
      categories. This method provides the opportunity to assess different binning approaches in both standard
      single-factor and multi-factor analyses.
      The main logic behind constructing nested dummy variables is to assign a value of 0 to all categories
      below the selected one and 1 to the selected category and all categories above it. Once constructed,
      their use in linear regression is equivalent to one-hot encoding in terms of the number of variables
      included, but the interpretation differs significantly.

Simulation Study

The purpose of this simulation study is to demonstrate the differences and similarities in linear regression output
when applying different encoding methods. In addition to differences in the technical output, practitioners
should be aware of the varying interpretations of the estimated coefficients associated with risk factors or
dummy variables, as well as the interpretation of the p-value when it is used as one of the criteria for selecting
the final model.

Conclusions


   In credit risk modeling, where practitioners assume a specific relationship between risk
   factors and the modeled target, the encoding of categorical risk factors should not be
   treated as a mechanical step.
   Practitioners should aim to align the different modeling steps throughout the model
   development process, including ensuring that the choice of encoding method is consistent
   with the risk factor engineering process. Moreover, they should carefully examine the
   transition from single-factor to multi-factor analysis and assess the potential impact of
   discrepancies between model estimates and the a priori expectations regarding the
   relationship between the risk factor and the target variable.

   Practitioners should be aware that the interpretation of regression coefficients depends on
   the selected encoding method.
   Practitioners should also understand the meaning of the p-values associated with regression
   coefficients under different encoding methods and treat them accordingly when designing
   the final model selection criteria.
