Margin of Conservatism in Probability of Default Modeling


    After completing Probability of Default (PD) model development, practitioners typically
    address overall model uncertainty by defining a so-called Margin of Conservatism (MoC).
    The European Banking Authority (EBA) defines three categories corresponding to distinct
    sources of uncertainty in the estimated parameters:
       1   Type A: uncertainty related to data and methodological deficiencies;
       2   Type B: uncertainty related to changes in risk appetite and internal processes;
       3   Type C: general estimation error, i.e., statistical uncertainty arising from parameter
           estimation.
    One of the most commonly applied MoC Type C relates to uncertainty in estimating the
    long-run average default rate (central tendency).
    According to the EBA PD & LGD Guidelines, for the purpose of determining PD estimates
    in the calibration process, institutions may consider either the long-run average default rate
    at the grade (pool) level or at the calibration segment level. Accordingly, a MoC Type C is
    expected to be quantified.

ECB Interpretation of MoC Type C


   In line with the EBA’s requirements, the ECB, in its Guide to Internal Models (2025),
   paragraph 327, section Model-Related MoC, clarifies its understanding of the quantification
   of MoC Type C, emphasizing that specific sources of uncertainty should reflect the
   dispersion of statistical estimators.
   Furthermore, the ECB distinguishes between models that output rating grades or pools and
   models with continuous outputs.
   As this presentation focuses on the most common approach in PD modeling, where the
   final model output is a rating scale, the remainder is dedicated to the ECB’s interpretation
   in this context.

   For models with rating grade outputs, the ECB emphasizes two main sources of uncertainty:
      1   the average of one-year default rates over time;
      2   the number of exposures per rating grade and per year, which affects the uncertainty
          of the one-year default rate.

ECB Interpretation of MoC Type C cont.

        Additionally, with regard to the average of one-year default rates, the ECB notes that
        dependency between default rates over time should be considered in the MoC quantification
        process.

Notes

        Given these clarifications from the ECB, and taking into account relatively short time series
        as well as specific statistical constraints in estimating the dependency structure, it is
        questionable how accurately all these sources can be incorporated into the quantification of
        MoC Type C.

        The purpose of this presentation is to emphasize the bias arising from the statistical
        identification of the dependency structure of default rates over time and to demonstrate
        which of the ECB-identified sources of uncertainty may contribute more to the final MoC
        quantification. Moreover, the proposed simulation framework can be straightforwardly
        extended to a model-based MoC Type C quantification tool, which may be particularly
        appropriate for calibrating rating grades based on the portfolio long-run average.

Challenges in Quantifying MoC Type C


   The ECB has set clear expectations for MoC Type C quantification, stating that the fewer
   the observations per grade and the shorter the time series, the higher the MoC assigned to
   that grade should be. Additionally, the ECB expects that the correlation between yearly
   default rates is properly addressed in this process.
   In practice, institutions often use simplified frameworks for MoC Type C quantification,
   typically neglecting either the correlation structure of default rates over time, the variability
   within specific reference dates, or both. However, should this be considered regulatory
   non-compliance explicitly?
   Given the limited number of observations of default rates over time, a key question is
   whether practitioners can accurately identify the correlation structure of defaults over time.
   Moreover, even with 100 observation points, for example, the first-order autoregressive
   coefficient would still be biased when estimated using OLS or Yule-Walker estimators, which
   are commonly applied for this purpose. Furthermore, the bias also depends on the
   assumptions underlying the data-generating process.

Challenges in Quantifying MoC Type C cont.

   Additionally, uncertainty within the one-year default rate often contributes much less (or
   even negligible) than the uncertainty in the average one-year default rate over time, making
   it questionable whether simplifying the MoC Type C quantification framework by excluding
   this source of uncertainty is justified.
   On the one hand, even a mild correlation over time in one-year default rates can have a
   significant impact on the final MoC Type C level; on the other hand, in certain cases, its
   estimation is biased when commonly applied methods are used.
   The contribution of uncertainty within the one-year default rate is also related to the length
   of the time series used to calculate the average one-year default rate. It can be an
   important source of uncertainty when the number of observations per rating grade or
   calibration segment is low and the time series of one-year default rates is short; however,
   how often do practitioners encounter such situations in practice for a well-designed model?

   In summary, considering regulatory expectations and practical challenges, it is worth asking
   whether certain simplifications of the modeling framework should be tolerated, or whether
   practitioners can rely on specific assumptions when quantifying MoC Type C without being
   fully able to confirm their appropriateness.

Simulation Study
Simulation Purpose and Underlying Assumptions

The purpose of this simulation is to assess the uncertainty in estimating the first-order autocorrelation coefficient
and to compare the contributions of the two sources of uncertainty in MoC Type C identified by the ECB.

The simulation is based on a Monte Carlo approach under the following assumptions:

    1   One-year default rates follow the Vasicek distribution over a 20-year period (T = 20).
    2   Asset correlation is set to 0.10 (ρ = 0.10).
    3   The average PD is set to 0.05 (PD = 0.05).
    4   The systemic factor follows a first-order autoregressive process with coefficient 0.30 (ϕ = 0.30), given by:

                                                                 p
                                             zt,t≤T = ϕzt−1 +        1 − ϕ2 ϵt

        where zt,t=1 and ϵt are drawn from the standard normal distribution.
    5   The number of exposures per year ranges from 100 to 5,000 (N = [100, 200, 500, 1000, 5000]).
    6   Defaults within a given year follow a binomial distribution with a probability of success equal to the
        conditional PD given the realized systemic factor from step 4.


Practitioners should note that step 6 can be fully aligned with the Vasicek framework by simulating and
aggregating individual defaults using the asset return approach, conditional on the simulated systemic factor.
However, both approaches produce the same results, with the approach presented here being computationally
less intensive.

Simulation Study cont.
Simulation Design

The following steps describe the simulation procedure, given the assumptions explained in the previous slide:

   1   For the selected average PD, ρ, and autocorrelation structure of the systemic factor, simulate T values
       of conditional PDs from the Vasicek distribution. Additionally, store the simulated values of the systemic
       factor.
   2   Calculate the first-order autoregressive coefficient from the simulated systemic factor realizations over
       the period T from step 1 and store the results.
   3   Calculate the average of the simulated PDs from step 1.
   4   Based on the conditional PDs and number of exposures N, simulate the number of defaults and
       recalculate the PD for each year of period T .
   5   Calculate the average default rate as the mean of the PDs simulated in step 4.
   6   Using the Vasicek framework, calculate a new systemic factor from the simulated PDs in step 4 and
       estimate and store another first-order autoregressive coefficient.
   7   Repeat steps 1 to 5, 100,000 times and store the results.
   8   Compare the distribution of the simulated autocorrelation coefficients to the true value, as well as assess
       the contribution of the within-year default rate uncertainty to the overall uncertainty of the simulation at
       a 95% confidence level.


The difference between the calculated percentiles of the simulated average default rates (step 5) and the selected
average PD serves as a simulation of MoC Type C related to the uncertainty of the LRA DR (central tendency),
incorporating regulatory-required sources of uncertainty and the dependency structure of default rates.

Discussion Points


    The ECB has set clear expectations for MoC Type C quantification. Should these
    expectations be revisited to allow greater flexibility?
    In practice, institutions often use simplified frameworks for MoC Type C quantification,
    neglecting certain sources of uncertainty or the dependency of default rates over time.
    Should this always be treated as regulatory non-compliance?

    Given the limited observations and the statistical challenges of accurately estimating the
    dependency structure of default rates, should certain simplifications and assumptions be
    permitted?
    Model-based simulation designs, under specific assumptions, can be the most effective tool
    for MoC Type C quantification or as a model challenger, capturing all key factors that may
    influence the final MoC Type C level. Should this approach be more widely promoted and
    aligned with capital requirement calculations in IRB models?
